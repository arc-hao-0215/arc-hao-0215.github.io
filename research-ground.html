Opening proposition

The ground is not simply the setting of the archive. The ground itself is the archive.

The military landscape is an arrangement of visibility, concealment, supply and movement. Ruins preserve parts of this arrangement; missing structures and altered ground also carry evidence. The museum study asks how another cultural use can enter that record without making its different moments appear complete or simultaneous.

Research question

How can architecture translate the layered spatial memory of a military landscape into contemporary cultural life without reconstructing a single past or erasing the traces between them?

Read a palimpsest, not a single origin.

The supplied fortress investigation distinguishes structures, construction phases, alterations and survival. Regional Ming–Zheng and Qing histories provide the wider coastal context; they should not be assigned indiscriminately to every surviving wall. Japanese-period construction and extension, postwar military use, abandonment and contemporary reuse form further layers. [P1]

The research method separates documentary history from visible fabric. A missing building is recorded as an absence, not filled in to produce a seamless historical image. This distinction matters because a museum route can otherwise turn a complicated military landscape into a simplified scene.

Regional historyMing–Zheng and Qing coastal context; distinguish context from dating of fabric
Military construction and changeEmplacements, shelter, additions and altered circulation

Contemporary cultural useA legible new layer, without completing an imagined past

Three systems within one ground.

The fortress investigation organises military architecture into combat, habitation and communication spaces. Read together with topography, these categories explain how commanding views coexist with concealed rooms and protected routes. Water collection, storage and filtration also make the site legible as an inhabited system rather than only a set of weapons positions. [P1]

This is a reading of a published survey, not a claim to have produced its measured drawings. The design research then asks which spatial relationships can continue: a route along a trench, an outlook from high ground, a sheltered threshold, or the relationship between an underground room and daylight.

Inherited systemSpatial logicContemporary question
Combat / observationHigh ground, exposure and directed viewsCan looking become reflection rather than surveillance?
Habitation / supportShelter, water and protected occupationWhat makes an interior inhabitable in this exposed landscape?
Communication / circulationRoutes linking dispersed spacesCan a cultural route preserve the legibility of the network?

Preserve, reuse, translate.

Fu Chao-ching’s discussion of heritage translation distinguishes conserving fabric, reactivating use and interpreting inherited cultural relationships. It supplies a conceptual lens for this study, rather than a licence to remove inconvenient historical evidence. [P2]

In the museum proposal, preservation retains significant traces; adaptive reuse gives existing spaces another use; translation carries spatial relationships into new architecture. The three operations may meet within a single route, but they answer different questions. The museum becomes another historical layer, not a replacement for the military landscape.

Preservation is not the completion of the past. It is the careful addition of another layer.

The section turns evidence into experience.

The design script begins at the former support area, then follows a sequence through partly buried galleries, reused emplacement spaces and a tunnel toward the southern sea view. Courts orient the interior toward the battery and supply indirect daylight. Changes of level and carefully framed views translate the existing ground into a sequence of encounters. [P3]

Different light conditions accompany different works in the Zhao Erdai collection. This is an architectural interpretation: compression, darkness, release and horizon propose ways of reading the site bodily. They do not claim to reproduce what its former occupants experienced.

Keep the archive open.

Conservation suitability cannot be established by a compelling section alone. Archaeological investigation, structural condition, coastal weathering, drainage and accessibility require further assessment. Water systems described in the investigation should be conserved or interpreted on the basis of their verified condition, not assumed to function unchanged.

The investigation and adaptive-reuse report remain independent evidence sources. The student proposal adds an interpretive argument to them; it does not replace a conservation plan or prove future visitor response. Its unresolved question is how to support new cultural life while allowing the site’s gaps and contradictions to remain visible.
