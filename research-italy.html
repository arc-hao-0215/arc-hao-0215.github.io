Research / Enquiry 02

The Trail Has to Earn Its Keep

Walking, production and the everyday economy of the Via Francigena

Argument

A restored path can remain economically peripheral. The architectural question is what gives residents a recurring reason to use and maintain the territory around it.

In April 2026, I joined the Tsinghua University–Politecnico di Torino field investigation in Valle d’Aosta. The Italian work formed a counterpart to research along the Tea Horse Trail in Yunnan. It reframed the route as a way to read connections among settlements, production, landscape and services, with architecture operating between those scales.

Comparative method

Roberto Dini and the joint Alpine programme · Read routes through inhabitation

Research question

How can architecture help a historic route sustain contemporary rural life?

Ancient trails as contemporary territory.

The joint programme connected the Tea Horse Trail and Via Francigena through the question of rural reactivation. The Alpine investigation included Aosta, Courmayeur, Saint-Rhémy-en-Bosses, Étroubles, Saint-Oyen and the Great St Bernard Pass. Encounters with local administrations, stakeholders and craftspeople placed everyday inhabitation within the research frame. [I1]

The comparison begins with a distinction: conserving the physical route and sustaining its surrounding territory are related but different tasks. A path may remain visible even as the services, livelihoods and social relationships that once supported it change. Architecture must therefore ask what makes passage useful to the places through which it passes.

Walking as a method of reading relationships.

Slow movement makes distance, slope and thresholds part of architectural observation. A settlement is encountered through its approaches, service spaces, productive edges and places to stop. The method is to relate these local conditions to the wider network: a building’s capacity for reuse depends partly on what can reach it, who needs it and which activities can persist around it.

This reading does not treat a scenic view as evidence of a functioning rural economy. Photographs and route maps show spatial relations; questions about ownership, seasonal occupation or economic viability require other forms of enquiry. Keeping those forms of evidence distinct prevents a field essay from turning an attractive landscape into an uncomplicated model.

Walk the service route as well as the visitor route

The joint research programme, with Roberto Dini, Lidia Preti, Martijn de Geus and Yi Shen, provides a comparative frame in which Alpine routes are examined through contemporary rural life. I use that frame to move from a scenic itinerary toward a transect of work: arrival, supply, making, storage, exchange and departure. This is the method developed in this essay, rather than a quotation of a participant’s position. [I1]

Walking reveals the effort between destinations. A map can place two buildings close together while a steep approach, a closed gate or a loading requirement makes their practical relation difficult. The relevant section therefore includes the service edge and the stopping place, not only the panorama. The same journey should be read from the position of someone carrying supplies and someone visiting without a burden.

That distinction is especially useful when examining reuse. An empty room is not automatically available capacity: it may be poorly connected to the activity expected to support it. The field observations establish physical questions. Interviews with operators and recurring seasonal records would be needed to establish whether the proposed connection is viable.

Production gives the route another reason to be used.

The research presentation places the Via Francigena beside food-producing workshops and spaces of storage. These encounters matter because production is both a livelihood and a way for visitors to understand a territory. The stopping place becomes an interface between movement and work rather than merely an amenity added to a tourist itinerary.

The architectural consequence is a network question: where can paths, workshops, shared rooms and rest facilities reinforce one another? In Yunnan, this returns as the relationship between tea production, the ancient garden and accommodation. The comparison concerns how activities connect; it does not propose transferring Alpine forms into the tea landscape.

The missing drawing is a working calendar

Food production brings different durations into the trail. Making, maturing, storing, selling and visiting do not occupy the same hours or seasons. A room serving more than one of these activities needs a negotiated timetable as much as a flexible plan. Public access may be appropriate at the edge of production while remaining incompatible with a clean work zone or a delivery period.

I propose reading a stopping place against three calendars: the resident’s ordinary week, the production cycle and the visitor season. Where all three coincide, shared facilities may have a strong everyday base. Where only the visitor season activates a building, the project risks adding another maintenance obligation to a territory with intermittent revenue. The observations do not yet establish an operating balance; the calendar is the next evidence needed to test it.

This makes modest interventions consequential. A covered waiting place, accessible store or shared washing facility may support an existing cycle more reliably than a new exhibition room. Architectural value can be assessed through the work made easier and the relationships kept in use, alongside the experience offered to a visitor.

Tourism is one condition of inhabitation.

Courmayeur complicates a simple narrative of rural decline. A territory can attract substantial visitor activity while still raising questions about housing, services and the continuity of permanent community life. Here these are research questions, not a claim that the field visit established household displacement or measured seasonal vacancy.

For architecture, the useful distinction is between an intervention that draws attention to a place and one that expands its capacity to be lived in. Reuse, everyday services and shared facilities deserve to be considered alongside visitor accommodation. Their value may be less spectacular but more continuous.

Attention is not the same as livelihood

Tourism can give a workshop an audience while changing what it makes, when it opens and whose needs organise the space. The productive encounter can become a staged demonstration. This is a tension to examine, not an accusation about the visited businesses. A comparative study should ask whether visitors supplement an existing economy or gradually become its condition of survival.

A counterfactual makes the distinction concrete: if visitor numbers fell for a season, which part of the proposed intervention would still be useful to residents? The answer may identify the most durable element of the brief. It also challenges the assumption that a stronger visual identity is sufficient to reactivate a rural place. A recognisable destination and an inhabitable territory are different achievements.

From Yunnan to the Alps.

The ICOMOS Cultural Routes Charter helps situate routes within reciprocal exchange and connected heritage. The two territories, however, remain historically and institutionally different. Comparison is productive when it reveals a common question while preserving those differences. [I3]

Architecture after the fieldwork.

The programme moved from investigation to discussion and exhibition at Castello del Valentino, Turin, on 14 April 2026. This dissemination stage brought student work from the two institutions into one comparative setting. [I2]

My architectural position is to start with relationships that already give a territory its life: production, access, care and recurring use. A new intervention should strengthen selected connections within that system. The Gaoligong project tests this through different degrees of presence, from more collective facilities near accessible ground to lighter structures along the mountain route.

A trail becomes a living system when movement sustains the places it connects.
