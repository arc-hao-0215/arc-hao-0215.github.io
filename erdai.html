Disarming the Landscape

Erdai Art Museum & Military Heritage Reuse

Fenggui Peninsula, PenghuHeritage · Memory · LandscapeAcademic Studio
Project proposition

How can a landscape built for defense become a place for art and reflection?

On Penghu's Fenggui Peninsula, the Dashan battery remains embedded in a coastal terrain of stone structures, trenches, bunkers and grass-covered emplacements. Once organised around concealment and military control, these spaces offer the possibility of a different public life.

Disarming the Landscape proposes the Erdai Art Museum as a new cultural layer within the fortress. Rather than replacing the remains with a freestanding object, partly buried galleries connect existing structures, courtyards and outdoor art spaces. Light, enclosure and framed views become the means of moving between inherited memory and contemporary use.

01 / Context

A landscape built for defense.

The battery was not a single building but a distributed defensive system. Access trenches, firing positions, observation points and support structures occupy the coastal terrain according to military logics of movement, visibility and protection. These surviving fragments, rather than an empty site, are the starting point of the design.

Reading the remains

Six inherited military elements establish the site's spatial organisation. Their different orientations, levels and material conditions suggest where circulation can be reopened and where a new public use might be introduced.

The proposal treats the existing fortifications as legible historical layers. New architectural insertions do not attempt to recreate an untouched fortress or visually erase the passage of time.

02 / Design approach

Preserving the trace. Transforming the purpose.

The project reinterprets the organisation of military infrastructure as a public sequence. Former access routes become part of a visitor journey; adapted emplacements host outdoor art; new galleries connect what had once been separated by defensive functions.

Three operations
01

Recognise the inherited ground.

Keep stone walls, military traces and the contour of the hillside visible as independent historic layers.

02

Open a public route.

Join old and new spaces through galleries, passages, courts and shared cultural facilities.

03

Bring light into the terrain.

Use skylights, planted courts and framed horizons to turn enclosure into spatial experience.

03 / Architecture

Between earth and light.

The architectural intervention follows the contours rather than rising above them. Galleries for different art forms are embedded in the terrain, punctuated by gardens and daylight, and connected to surviving military structures through a sequence of changing thresholds.

Drawing views

Overall plan and longitudinal section

Plan. The museum links rooms for painting, photography, ceramics, calligraphy, sculpture and moving images. Each offers a different spatial condition rather than one undifferentiated exhibition hall.

Section. Recessed rooms, level changes and light wells establish a continuing dialogue between the hillside above and the galleries below.

04 / Spatial experience

A journey through memory.

A sequence of public thresholds, enclosed passages and daylit rooms translates the site's former conditions of protection and concealment into new encounters with art. The images below are arranged as a curatorial reading of the project, rather than a claim of an exact room-by-room walking itinerary.

A / Arrival

Re-entering the battery.

Contemporary public space joins the existing stonework, offering an accessible cultural threshold without disguising the site's history.

B / Concealment

Entering the ground.

Lower ceilings and controlled light frame photography and moving images, recalling the sensation of enclosure without reproducing the original military use.

C / Encounter

Art within the earth.

An expansive painting room contrasts with a linear calligraphy passage, giving different art forms their own rhythm of viewing.

D / Light

Moments of release.

Garden courtyards, overhead openings, taller rooms and long apertures interrupt the subterranean journey. Natural light marks a transition from concentrated viewing toward the landscape beyond.

E / Landscape

Art returns to the open air.

Outdoor works are encountered beside inherited stone boundaries and coastal vegetation. A defensive ground becomes a place for shared exploration.

Scope & development

Disarming the Landscape is an unbuilt academic proposal. Archaeological evidence, structural condition, coastal drainage, conservation requirements and barrier-free circulation would require coordinated assessment before implementation.
