Culture After the Ribbon-Cutting

Who makes a district public after the architect leaves?

Argument

The opening of a cultural building is one event. Its civic value is made repeatedly through access, tenancy, maintenance and the freedom of people to use the spaces around it.

I joined the third Bi-City Youth Cultural Leadership Programme expecting to learn about museums and cultural institutions. The experience expanded into a question about how culture is produced by a city. Encounters with Swire Properties were especially influential: they made the decisions between design and daily life—management, programming, tenancy, maintenance and public access—part of my architectural enquiry.

Comparative method

Atelier Bow-Wow · Read urban life through recurring situations

Research question

How do architecture, development and everyday stewardship produce a cultural ecosystem?

Culture depends on relationships.

The 2024 programme brought sixteen university students from Hong Kong and Beijing into a two-month exchange across Hong Kong, Chengdu and Beijing under the theme “The Future of Cultural Ecosystems.” Workshops, visits and institutional encounters placed different forms of cultural production in relation. [H1]

Hong Kong became the principal territory of my reflection. A museum needs audiences, educators, public space and infrastructure; a neighbourhood depends on businesses, residents and accumulated memories. A place to gather needs maintenance and repeated occupation. The word ecosystem becomes useful when it shifts attention from an isolated cultural object to the relationships that allow it to act in the city.

The developer as an urban actor.

My exchanges with Swire Properties challenged a narrow understanding of developers as actors who finance and deliver buildings. The programme presented placemaking as an ongoing relationship among art, local culture, development and management. Swire’s published account makes this institutional ambition explicit. [H2]

For me, the important shift was temporal. An architect may be involved through a defined period of design and delivery; an owner or developer may continue shaping a district for decades. Tenant selection, access rules, landscape care and cultural programming can change the meaning of a place as profoundly as its initial plan. Development therefore becomes a subject of architectural research, rather than only a condition outside it.

Control can be mapped as carefully as circulation

The encounter with development practice changed the scale of the brief for me. Instead of ending with a completed space, I began tracing the decisions that continually produce it: who selects tenants, approves events, maintains planting, changes opening hours and responds to a conflict over use. These decisions do not necessarily belong to the same actor.

A spatially continuous district can therefore contain discontinuous rights. Walking across a plaza, remaining there and organising an activity may be governed differently. A future study should map those permissions beside the pedestrian network. This would allow generous physical provision and restrictive use rules to be assessed together, rather than assuming one cancels out the other.

The comparison with ordinary neighbourhood space is not a claim that unmanaged conditions are automatically more inclusive. Informal territories also have exclusions and hierarchies. The question is whether people can understand, negotiate and influence the rules through which either kind of space operates.

A generous plaza raises questions of control.

At developments such as Taikoo Place, I began asking how the project meets the existing city and what happens between buildings. Can someone remain without shopping? Can a small cultural organisation participate? How does a place change over time, and who decides which activities belong there?

A privately governed space can support substantial public life while retaining rules that shape access and expression. That tension is not resolved by an attractive ground floor or the presence of public art. It calls for a more precise account of publicness: who can enter, who can stay, who can organise an activity and who can contest a decision.

Walking beyond the curated development.

Neighbourhood streets and housing estates offered a counterpoint to professional placemaking. Corridors, podiums, markets and small shops showed how use accumulates through repeated ordinary decisions. A designed framework is continually interpreted by the people who inhabit it.

Walking became a way to examine transitions rather than collect landmarks: station to shopping centre, housing estate to market, temple to infrastructure, old neighbourhood to redevelopment. The question was what connects these settings and which forms of everyday life are supported at their edges. This reading does not turn every informal use into an ideal; it asks what a managed project can learn from continuity that it did not centrally design.

Draw a situation rather than a landmark

Atelier Bow-Wow’s research, including Made in Tokyo and Pet Architecture, examines urban combinations that conventional building categories can overlook. I use this as a comparative method for attending to relationships between use and setting. It sharpens the Hong Kong reflection without recasting a personal walk as a completed ethnographic survey. [H3]

The research unit becomes a recurring situation: someone waiting where a passage widens, a shop edge that supports conversation, a route between a housing estate and a market. Its drawing would include the activity, physical support, time and governing rule. The same bench can support a brief pause, a daily meeting or a managed event; the difference lies partly beyond its form.

Repetition matters because an exceptional festival and an ordinary weekday reveal different capacities. A proper next stage would revisit selected transitions at comparable times and record what changes. The present reflection identifies those situations as questions; it does not supply invented counts, interviews or claims about all residents.

Chengdu and Beijing sharpened the question.

The programme’s Chengdu visits connected cultural institutions with Taikoo Li Chengdu and its relationship to the Daci Temple. The official account identifies heritage, art and commercial development as explicit subjects of the exchange. [H1] [H2]

The resulting question for me was what preservation retains: fabric, urban morphology, cultural practices, memory or an image of history. Beijing shifted the frame toward institutional conservation and the communication of heritage. Hong Kong remained the place where I could bring these scales back together, because institutions, commerce, infrastructure and neighbourhood life are so closely interwoven.

From a cultural ecosystem to a shared record.

Their Story, the Eastern District community calendar, provided a smaller scale for this enquiry. Local stories become material that can be edited, designed and circulated, asking whose everyday experience is visible within the public image of a place. The project extends architectural attention from built form to cultural representation.

A printed calendar cannot stand in for a neighbourhood or resolve its competing interests. Its value lies in making specific stories available for shared attention. It also places responsibility on the editor: selection and presentation should remain attentive to the people represented.

Who gets to become the story of a place?

A community calendar makes this question tangible. Editorial selection gives some experiences visibility and leaves others outside the frame. The design work must distinguish a person’s account from the editor’s interpretation, and an individual story from a claim about a whole neighbourhood. The calendar is a situated cultural object, not a substitute for collective representation.

This brings the larger discussion of placemaking back to a modest scale. Cultural value is not produced only by commissioning prominent art or programming an event. It also depends on the means through which people can describe their own place, disagree with its public image and keep a record that lasts beyond a campaign. Stewardship includes who can revise that record.

Design continues through stewardship.

The most consequential change in my thinking was to look at actors and time alongside space. Urban quality depends on a continuing process of adaptation, programming, investment and care. Culture can shape development early, but it can also become branding if the ability of people to participate is left unexamined.

I now approach a project with a larger set of questions: who owns and manages it, who uses it, who remembers what was there before and what happens after the opening photographs. The enquiry remains deliberately open about the relationship between commercial value and cultural value. The task is to make their negotiation visible and ask how it can become more inclusive.

Architecture creates space; stewardship shapes what a place can continue to become.
