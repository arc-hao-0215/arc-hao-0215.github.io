Research / Enquiry 10

The Public Floor

Can an elevated library extend the city’s common ground?

Argument

Suspending a library can release the ground. Whether that ground becomes public depends on its connections, its uses and the rules that remain after the architectural gesture.

Between Chang’an Avenue and the hutongs, the library study treats the section as a research instrument. Ground, suspended volumes, gardens and bridges are read together to test how a place of knowledge can remain connected to the city through movement, shared use and environmental exchange.

Comparative method

Herman Hertzberger · Read institutions through thresholds and appropriation

Research question

How can the section of a library connect urban access, collective learning and environmental systems into a continuous civic interior?

The institution and the ground it occupies.

The portfolio frames the site through the relationship between Chang’an Avenue and the hutongs. A library here is not only a destination for readers. Its edge and ground floor affect how different urban conditions meet. The study asks whether the institution can provide a shared route and civic room as well as a collection of interior programmes.

The enquiry uses urban analysis, sectional drawings and physical models to test spatial relationships. The IFLA–UNESCO Public Library Manifesto offers an institutional frame: the library’s public purpose extends across education, culture, information and inclusion. Architecture must turn that ambition into accessible rooms, routes and usable time. [B2]

From the common hall to the metropolitan interior

Hertzberger describes the classrooms of the Delft Montessori school as identifiable places along a shared hall. The comparison helps distinguish collective space from an undifferentiated open plan: people need a place from which to participate, as well as a space in which to meet. [B3]

Applied to the library, this suggests examining the edges between reading, circulation, discussion and retreat. A large continuous interior can be visually democratic while making it difficult to concentrate or to occupy a place without feeling exposed. Publicness requires options at different scales: the individual seat, the small group, the shared route and the institution as a whole.

The research therefore reads the section as a set of invitations and separations. A bridge is more than a connection if it offers somewhere to pause without obstructing movement. A garden contributes to shared use only if its approach, seating and maintenance allow it to be occupied. These relationships can be tested without assuming that maximum openness is always desirable.

Read one section through four systems.

The sectional perspective is examined successively as space, structure, climate and programme. Each reading asks what enables or interrupts continuity. Physical models test the relation between suspended volumes, support, light and the ground plane. This shared section makes the systems comparable without pretending they operate independently.

Civic continuity has a vertical dimension.

The section suggests that public space need not stop at the ground floor. Bridges, gardens and reading areas extend the possibility of shared occupation upward. The architectural finding is a proposition about continuity: different rooms can contribute to a public institution when the routes between them are treated as meaningful civic space.

Continuity is more demanding than visual openness. A route must also be understandable, accessible and usable under the institution’s operating rules. The drawings establish a spatial possibility; they do not establish equal access, freedom from exclusion or a particular pattern of use.

The civic value of a room depends partly on the relationships that make it accessible.

Follow the person who did not come for a book

A civic library also receives someone seeking a quiet place, a meeting, information or a route through the district. To test the public floor, I trace an arrival from the street through entry, orientation, vertical movement and a destination. The chain is only as accessible as its most restrictive threshold. A generous room at the top cannot compensate for an uncertain entrance below.

This reading distinguishes an urban shortcut from admission to the institution. They may share a ground plane while requiring different hours and degrees of supervision. The design should make that boundary explicit, so that closing a reading area does not unnecessarily close the common route. The drawings suggest a spatial opportunity; the operating brief would have to secure it.

A second chain follows a person who cannot use the prominent stair. Equivalent access involves more than providing a lift somewhere in the building. It includes legibility, distance, continuity and arrival at the same civic places. These are criteria for the next design test, not certified results of the present scheme.

Structure makes a public promise.

Suspending volumes changes the relation between occupied space and the city below. The truss and modular grid are therefore studied as civic decisions as well as structural ones: they influence which parts of the ground can remain permeable and how shared interiors meet across the section.

The physical model provides a second reading of that promise. It tests the proximity of volumes, the depth of light and the experience of openings. Its value is comparative and spatial; load paths, movement, fire strategy and long-span feasibility would need separate technical verification.

Learning space as an environmental relationship.

Roof energy systems, cross-atrium ventilation, an adaptive envelope, rainwater capture and interior gardens are located within the same sectional argument. The environmental systems are not presented as a separate list of sustainable features: the study asks how they shape places for reading, movement and gathering.

This coupling creates an architectural question rather than a proven efficiency claim. Ventilation and daylight may support public occupation in one part of the building while producing heat, glare or acoustic conflicts elsewhere. The section makes those dependencies available for testing.

A system drawing must also show disagreement

The four readings of the section are most useful where they conflict. An atrium may connect floors while transmitting noise. A glazed edge can admit daylight and produce glare. A planted interior needs water and maintenance routes beside places intended for books and sustained concentration. Suspending a volume opens space below while increasing the demands on structure and servicing.

Rather than presenting those systems as automatically complementary, the research identifies the interfaces that require a choice. Which reading areas need acoustic separation? Where should shading protect occupation? Can garden maintenance occur without closing the public route? The architectural argument becomes stronger when its celebrated sectional continuity can survive these ordinary demands.

A public building needs an operating model.

The unresolved questions include universal access, opening hours, security thresholds, acoustic separation, wayfinding and the maintenance of shared gardens and environmental systems. None can be answered by a sectional image alone.

The next stage would compare the proposal against access scenarios, environmental simulation and institutional requirements. This study contributes an integrated way of asking those questions: collective knowledge depends on the spaces between programmes, the support beneath them and the rules through which people can use them.
