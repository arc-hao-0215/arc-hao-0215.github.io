Research / Enquiry 08

Whose Waterfront?

Access, residential thresholds and the uneven benefits of urban renewal

Argument

Opening a route to the lake can enlarge public life and intensify pressure on the people living beside it. The design must specify whose access improves, and on what terms.

Waterfront+ begins with discontinuities around the Fire God Temple, Di’anmen and Baimi Xiejie: between water and hutongs, commercial frontages and residential space, historic landmarks and everyday routes. The research treats these breaks as different kinds of permeability to be repaired, rather than a shoreline to be opened uniformly.

Comparative method

Zhang Ke / ZAO–standardarchitecture · Work through the occupied courtyard

Research question

How can a historic waterfront become an urban seam that reconnects water, neighbourhood life, cultural memory and contemporary commerce without reducing the district to a tourist landscape?

Water changes its civic role.

The project script traces changing uses of water around the site: transport, gardens, swimming, tourist boating and winter ice activity. The relation between water and city is therefore historical and seasonal. What appears today as an edge has also been a route, a workplace, a destination and a shared civic setting.

The team’s drawings locate cultural, spatial and historical discontinuities within this changing landscape. The design problem is not simply insufficient waterfront access. It is the weakening of connections between local identity, residential life, commerce and the historic city.

Different evidence answers different questions.

Published research by Liu Yifei and colleagues used 30 GoPro participants and extracted 25,089 images to examine visual perception along the Shichahai waterfront. Their study found stronger perception of landmarks than edges and paths, and argued for coherent views and visual corridors. These data belong to that published study; they were not collected by the Waterfront+ team. [S2]

Shao Lei’s research on resident differentiation shows why “the community” cannot be treated as one uniform constituency: economic circumstances, housing conditions and preferences for change differ. Bian Lanchun and Jing Zhongjie’s conservation work offers a complementary method, using protection frameworks and courtyard-level records and guidance. Together, these sources help distinguish visual legibility, social needs and the unit of physical intervention. [S3] [S4]

This literature informs the present research reading. It is not presented as proof that the design’s original process implemented each published method or that earlier survey findings describe current residents unchanged.

Begin with an occupied unit, not an empty diagram

Zhang Ke’s Hutong Children’s Library & Art Centre provides a useful comparison because a small shared programme is placed within an inherited courtyard condition. The relevant method is close attention to what can coexist at that scale. The precedent does not demonstrate that an entire waterfront district will respond in the same way. [S5]

For Waterfront+, the courtyard is both a physical unit and a set of thresholds among people. Its entrance, circulation and shared surfaces cannot be read as vacant capacity merely because they appear open on a plan. A proposed passage may cross a place of domestic work, waiting or informal supervision. These relationships require observation and negotiation before they can become a public route.

The implication is to redraw the district as a series of differentiated permissions. Some connections can be public through-routes; others should be shared locally, open at certain times or remain private. The design gains precision when it can explain a retained boundary as clearly as a new opening.

Four permeabilities; three publics.

The project’s own analysis compares urban form, transport, building character, condition, public space and programme. Cross-reading character with condition informs decisions to retain, repair, adapt, open or insert. A courtyard provides an incremental unit; linked routes and water-facing spaces allow interventions to operate at the scale of point, line and area.

The research is organised here through four kinds of permeability. They are a framework for evaluating the project drawings, not four measured performance scores.

PermeabilityQuestionDrawing or programme to examine
PhysicalCan everyday routes connect water and hutong?Pedestrian links, thresholds and crossings
VisualCan landmarks and water remain legible from within the district?View corridors and framed openings
ProgrammaticDo uses connect across the edge?Library, community rooms, markets and shared dining
SocialWho can occupy the space, and on what terms?Resident-oriented, shared and visitor-oriented provision

An opening is not yet a public relationship.

The combined reading suggests that physical access is necessary but insufficient. A newly opened passage may still serve only visitor consumption. A clear view of the water may coexist with a loss of affordable everyday uses. The distinction between resident-oriented, shared and visitor-oriented programmes therefore becomes central to evaluating an intervention.

The drawings propose a community library, activity centre, shared dining, public landscape and commercial interfaces. These should be judged together: do they sustain ordinary use through the day and across seasons, and do they give residents reasons to remain? Those questions extend the design beyond the number of openings along a water-facing facade.

The aim is not simply to bring people closer to the water, but to bring the water back into the everyday structure of the city.

Publicness has a timetable and a cost

The resident returning home, the child going to an activity room and the visitor following the lake share parts of the same network but do not ask the same things of it. Their routes differ in repetition, time, speed and tolerance for crowding. A continuous promenade can improve visitor orientation while making an ordinary trip longer or noisier for a resident.

A useful evaluation therefore follows these journeys separately through the proposed plan. It asks which doors must remain open, which facilities can be reached without a purchase and which uses survive outside peak visitor hours. This is a proposed test of the design, not a claim to have measured its outcomes.

Commercial activity may finance maintenance and attract use, but it can also make free occupation conditional. The proposal’s community library, dining and activity spaces should be assessed through access rules and running costs, not only their programme labels. A room called “community” has not yet established who can book it, who can enter or who pays to keep it available.

Waterfront+ as a sequence of repairs.

The project script works through existing nodes rather than a single continuous object: Yandai Xiejie, Di’anmen’s commercial block, the Fire God Temple and the Baimi Xiejie neighbourhood. Retaining useful structure, adapting courtyards, adjusting circulation and inserting shared spaces carry the waterfront into the district. The strategy diagrams connect removal and preservation, transport, public nodes and landscape views.

At the neighbourhood scale, the courtyard becomes a unit for gradual change. At the urban scale, paths and view corridors reconnect larger systems. The section tests how water-facing public life can coexist with the depth of the hutong fabric, rather than replace it with a thin tourist frontage.

Commercial vitality without a tourist monoculture.

A staged renewal strategy should begin with interventions that can improve ordinary access and shared use without depending on a complete commercial redevelopment. Each stage would need a record of affected uses, conservation constraints and the people responsible for operation. The courtyard unit makes such staging imaginable; it does not remove the need to negotiate ownership and occupancy.

The project does not demonstrate that spatial improvements prevent displacement. Tenure, affordability, business continuity and the distribution of commercial benefit remain decisive. A credible assessment would compare conditions before and after each stage, including who remains and who gains access, rather than treating greater visitor activity as a sufficient measure of success.

The research conclusion is selective permeability: open the connections that support shared life, retain the thresholds that make inhabitation possible, and attach an operating commitment to every new public room.
