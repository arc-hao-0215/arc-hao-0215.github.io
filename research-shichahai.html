Research / Enquiry 08

Whose Waterfront?

Public access, everyday life and heritage conservation in a historic lake district

Context / Shichahai, Beijing

Shichahai is both an inhabited neighbourhood and a public image of historic Beijing. Its lakes, streets, courtyards and commercial frontages do not offer the same kind of access or belonging.

This enquiry examines how those differences came into being. Rather than treating the lakeshore as a continuous public promenade, it reads Shichahai as a historic water system embedded in everyday residential life, regulated heritage space and a changing visitor economy. The question of who uses the waterfront is inseparable from who lives immediately behind it, how buildings are occupied, and how public space is governed.

The reading combines historical records, conservation and management documents, published urban studies and the spatial context of a 2024 collaborative investigation. Evidence from other authors is attributed to those authors; it is not presented as original interviews, measured pedestrian counts or a current survey of residents.

Research question

How have heritage conservation, tourism development and everyday residential life shaped access to and occupation of Shichahai's historic waterfront?

01 / Historical geography

Water before the waterfront

The three connected lakes now known as Qianhai, Houhai and Xihai were once part of a larger system of urban water. In the Yuan dynasty, Jishuitan functioned as a northern terminus of the Grand Canal: an infrastructure of transport, supply and exchange rather than a picturesque landscape alone. The water's role changed as the city changed. During the Ming and Qing periods, the lakes also became settings for gardens, temples, seasonal recreation and social gatherings. [S1]

A 1983 municipal account describes a very different twentieth-century shoreline, recording deteriorated environmental conditions, occupied lakeside green space and paths interrupted by enclosures. Read as a historical document, it demonstrates that the apparent openness of a waterfront is not a fixed property of geography: it has repeatedly been altered by maintenance, land occupation and public administration. The account is evidence of conditions at the time, not a description of the district today. [S2]

These successive roles—port, residential setting, place of leisure and protected cultural landscape—continue to inform how the district is remembered and represented. Shichahai cannot be reduced to a single historical identity or one uninterrupted story of public access.

02 / Urban morphology

A shoreline of different boundaries

From the lakeside, Shichahai can appear to be a coherent scenic district. Moving inland changes that impression. Streets and hutongs, commercial frontages, courtyard entrances, temple precincts and domestic thresholds establish different relationships between the visible waterfront and the occupied city behind it. A line of sight towards water does not necessarily correspond to a route through the neighbourhood; a publicly passable street does not make its adjoining courtyard public.

These are distinctions of spatial interpretation rather than claims from a systematic access audit. They encourage a closer reading of entrances, street continuity, enclosure, frontage activity and the transition from collective circulation to residential space. Within such a fabric, an apparent gap on a map may be an inhabited threshold rather than unused land.

A published 2019 study of an approximately 800-metre route in the Jinsitao lakeside area used wearable-camera images from 30 volunteers to examine visual perception. Its material offers evidence about what walking observers notice along a specific route; it does not measure property rights, household needs or the whole district's accessibility. The visual prominence of water and landmarks therefore cannot stand in for the social meaning of access. [S3]

03 / Conservation and governance

Heritage as a lived condition

Shichahai was among the historic conservation areas identified in Beijing's 1999 programme for defining protected districts and their control boundaries. In this framework, preservation extends beyond individual monuments to the relationships among waterways, street patterns, traditional buildings and their setting. Conservation is therefore an urban question, not only a question of restoring facades. [S4]

The Xicheng District's 2014 management measures explicitly place residence and tourism alongside heritage protection. They address scenic resources, building uses, commercial activity and administrative responsibilities, and call for public consultation in planning. This is significant because the waterfront is not governed solely as open space: its everyday life is shaped by overlapping rules concerning dwellings, businesses, public conduct and historic fabric. [S5]

Policy documents establish stated responsibilities and permitted processes; they do not by themselves establish how effectively rules are enforced or how different residents experience them. The distinction between formal protection and lived conditions remains essential to reading a historic neighbourhood.

04 / Residential life

More than one public

The term “public waterfront” can obscure the fact that not everyone approaches the lake in the same way. For a nearby resident, the streets may form habitual routes between home, shops, work and local services. For a visitor, the same streets may form a short itinerary of landmarks, restaurants and views. For someone running a business, the lakeside is also a place of work. These perspectives overlap spatially without being interchangeable.

Courtyard housing makes that distinction particularly important. Entrances and shared circulation areas may accommodate neighbours' everyday interactions while still carrying expectations of privacy and domestic continuity. Their social role cannot be inferred from their geometry alone. Likewise, describing a district as a “community” does not establish that households have common incomes, tenure arrangements or attitudes towards change.

Research on Shichahai's cultural transformation has documented tensions between established local practices and newly introduced activities. Such findings draw attention to residents and social networks as part of the district's heritage; they should not be treated as a census of present-day households or as evidence that every street experiences identical pressures. [S6]

05 / Local economy and cultural change

Tourism and the changing street

Shichahai's tourist economy draws on precisely those qualities that also sustain local identity: waterside scenery, hutong morphology, historic sites and the atmosphere of ordinary urban life. Yet the activities that make a place legible to visitors are not identical to those that sustain residential routines. A street of visitor-oriented businesses may remain lively while offering fewer reasons for nearby residents to use it in their daily lives.

Chen Li and Zhang Wenzhong's 2010 study of local cultural change in Shichahai examined the effects of urban renewal and globalisation on traditional practices. Their findings describe a weakening of some established cultural characteristics and separations between introduced and local cultural forms at the time of study. This is valuable historical evidence of social and commercial transition, not a timeless verdict on the entire neighbourhood. [S6]

Planning records also show that tourism was not an incidental addition to Shichahai's recent development: municipal and district policies have explicitly addressed leisure functions, commercial uses, historic protection and residential order together. The resulting waterfront is simultaneously a neighbourhood setting, an economic destination and an object of public regulation. [S5]

06 / Everyday and seasonal time

The waterfront changes with time

Public space is not experienced only through permanent paths and boundaries. Shichahai has long supported activities that change with the season, from summer gatherings and waterside recreation to winter ice activities. Municipal histories present these recurring uses as part of the lakes' social life. What the same shore means can change across seasons, hours and groups of users. [S1] [S7]

This temporal dimension complicates the notion of a uniformly public edge. The ability to pass through a space, sit without purchasing anything or use a local facility may depend on opening hours, occupation, management and social expectations. These are questions for close observation and future empirical comparison; this enquiry does not claim to have measured them over a full day or year.

Looking at time alongside urban form allows everyday life to appear as something more than a static land-use category. The same location can accommodate quiet domestic movement, organised leisure and intense visitor activity without offering each group an equal experience.

Synthesis / Research findings

Uneven publicness

Across historical records, conservation documents and published social research, a consistent distinction emerges: the visibility of a waterfront is not equivalent to a shared right or ability to use it. Shichahai's public life is shaped by overlapping systems of access, residence, commerce, cultural recognition and management.

Three conclusions follow from the evidence. First, the lake's civic role is historically contingent: water has been infrastructure, a setting for local life and a destination for recreation at different moments. Second, the district's spatial boundaries have differing social meanings: a hutong, a courtyard and a lakeside route cannot be classified by openness alone. Third, preservation and tourism are not external forces acting upon an otherwise unchanged neighbourhood; they are among the institutions and economies through which its contemporary everyday life is organised.

A waterfront may be visible to everyone without being experienced in the same way by everyone.

“Whose waterfront?” is therefore not a question resolved by identifying one public. It asks which forms of belonging are made visible, which daily practices remain less apparent, and how historical value is negotiated between the scenic district and the inhabited city. The open question is empirical: how do these relationships vary among individual streets, households and seasons today?
