Spirited A Way

Kangrinboqê Kora Route — Pilgrimage Architecture & Landscape Design

Mount Kailash, NgariRitual · Landscape · Adaptive Reuse · Modular InfrastructureAcademic Studio · 2024
Project proposition

How much architecture does a sacred landscape need?

Mount Kailash is both an extraordinary mountain landscape and a sacred destination shared by multiple religious traditions. Along its pilgrimage circuit, the need for rest, sanitation, safety and interpretation must coexist with an environment shaped by faith and ecological sensitivity.

Spirited A Way proposes three architectural responses along the route: a renewed pilgrims’ inn, modular supply stations and a landscape-sensitive exhibition hall. Each explores how essential services can support the journey without becoming the landscape’s dominant presence.

01 / Landscape

Reading a sacred landscape.

The approximately 56-kilometre kora around Mount Kailash is shaped by ritual movement, sacred landmarks, exposed valleys and a highly seasonal mountain climate. The investigation connects territorial geography with the more intimate sequence of the pilgrimage route.

Landscape perception

Mapping where the journey gathers meaning.

A study of 1,729 geolocated social-media photographs mapped concentrations of attention along the route. Paths, edges, districts, nodes and landmarks reveal how movement, cultural landmarks and scenic views contribute to the perceived landscape.

Integrated site evaluation

Where cultural practices and climate shape the journey.

The three preceding mappings are brought together to evaluate conditions along the pilgrimage circuit. The study on the left identifies and assesses existing man-made artefacts, then traces how weather, seasonal changes and ritual practices influence pilgrimage behaviour. The map on the right synthesizes the findings into site typologies and environmental gradients.

The mappings inform the design questions and site selection; they are not evidence that any proposed intervention has been environmentally approved or constructed. Explore the separate research →

02 / Route strategy

Three responses within one landscape system.

Rather than placing identical structures at regular intervals, three proposals respond to distinct moments in the pilgrimage: renewed accommodation at First Tavern, modular rest and essential services along the route, and cultural interpretation near the ritual landscape. Their locations were considered through the wider mapping of terrain, climate, existing facilities and cultural practices.

03 / Accommodation

First TavernRenewing shelter within an existing footprint.

The First Tavern proposal reworks an existing pilgrim inn. Shared spaces, accommodation and circulation are reorganized around a protected courtyard, using renovation instead of a larger new building to support longer stays.

Adaptive accommodation

The courtyard becomes a point of return.

Existing accommodation and common facilities are reorganized around a sheltered courtyard rather than extending the building's footprint. A relief of the pilgrimage circuit turns the shared ground into a place to read and discuss the journey.

Stone, earth and timber maintain continuity with the surrounding built landscape.

Landscape setting

Accommodation within a larger journey.

The pilgrimage route is experienced through the movement of travellers, the established buildings and the expansive mountain landscape.

First Tavern / Ground-floor plan

A courtyard at the centre.

Reception, dining, accommodation and shared services are arranged within the existing footprint. The single ground-floor plan makes their relationship to the central courtyard easier to read.

04 / Modular infrastructure

Supply StationsShelter as a kit of parts.

Exposed stretches of the kora need essential services without the physical or operational footprint of a conventional hotel. The proposal develops prefabricated timber–aluminium units that can combine sleeping, dining, sanitation and service functions according to different stopping conditions.

Distributed support

Rest, replenish, continue.

Instead of concentrating services in one large building, repeatable modules can accommodate different stopping conditions along the route. Elevated supports, insulated enclosures and prefabricated components reduce the need for extensive site work.

Modular organization

Different uses, one construction logic.

Sleeping, dining and service rooms can be combined according to local capacity and seasonal needs.

Everyday occupation

From a bed to a shared table.

Compact overnight accommodation and collective dining share the same modular construction system.

Construction and servicing / 08 steps

From fabrication to deployment.

The eight-step diagram traces a repeatable supply chain, from off-site material preparation to a functioning shelter. The following English guide corresponds to the numbered stages and Chinese annotations in the drawing.

  1. ShippingTimber and components travel from the processing yard to the route.
  2. ManufactoryMaterials are cut and prepared as transportable building elements.
  3. StoragePrefabricated parts are stored for staged delivery and assembly.
  4. DeploymentA truck carries components for an individual shelter unit.
  5. StructureA lightweight prefabricated frame is assembled on site.
  6. Module Plug-inEnclosed functional modules are fitted into the frame.
  7. Facilities InstallationPhotovoltaics, access steps and service elements are added.
  8. Unit DuplicationThe same kit of parts is repeated where facilities are needed.
Building section / material layers

Layers designed for maintenance.

The section separates the energy-producing roof, protective envelope, service channels and elevated base into legible components. Each is designed to be fabricated or maintained independently, rather than treating the shelter as a single permanent mass.

01 / ROOFSolar and weather protection

Photovoltaic panels sit above a 3 mm profiled aluminium sheet, carried by a fire-resistant aluminium-alloy frame.

02 / ENVELOPEInsulation and prefabricated timber

Two 100 mm polyurethane-foam layers are shown with spruce wood panels to form the insulated enclosure.

05 / Exhibition

Sky Burial Exhibition HallKeeping distance from the sacred ground.

The exhibition hall is conceived as a restrained setting for learning about the cultural and spiritual landscape near the sky burial platform. It stays away from the ritual ground: the studio proposal sets a separation of more than 300 metres from significant ritual locations, subject to further cultural and site-specific evaluation.

The ritual landscape

The landscape comes before the building.

The proposed hall creates a separate place for orientation and interpretation. Sacred ground remains untouched by the visitor facilities; the experience begins with the mountain, the route and the significance of the ritual setting.

Architectural approach

A building drawn into the terrain.

The partially embedded hall brings exhibition, film and observation into a restrained sequence, avoiding a singular monument against the sacred mountain landscape.

Interior experience

Two ways of looking outward.

The long glazed exhibition gallery maintains an immediate connection to the mountain and the passing journey, keeping the sacred landscape present even while visitors pause inside.

A more enclosed terrain gallery shifts attention toward the spatial and cultural geography of the kora. A physical landscape model and carefully framed views allow the pilgrimage route to be studied from a respectful distance.

Scope & development

This project is an academic landscape architecture and facility-planning proposal, not an approved or completed intervention. Its diagrams and renders test relationships between site selection, ritual landscape, adaptive reuse, distributed shelter and construction logistics. Further development would require the involvement of religious custodians and local communities, as well as structural, ecological, hazard, service and operational assessment.
