Spirited A Way

Kangrinboqê Kora Route — Pilgrimage Architecture & Landscape Design

Mount Kailash, NgariRitual · Landscape · Adaptive Reuse · Modular InfrastructureAcademic Studio · 2024
Project proposition

How much architecture does a sacred landscape need?

Mount Kailash is both an extraordinary mountain landscape and a sacred destination shared by multiple religious traditions. Along its pilgrimage circuit, the need for rest, sanitation, safety and interpretation must coexist with an environment shaped by faith and ecological sensitivity.

Spirited A Way proposes a coordinated network of four architectural responses: an interpretation hall near a ritual setting, an extension to Dirapuk Monastery, an adapted pilgrims' inn and modular supply stations. Each intervention explores how essential services can support the journey without becoming the landscape's dominant presence.

01 / Landscape

Reading a sacred landscape.

The approximately 56-kilometre kora around Mount Kailash is shaped by ritual movement, sacred landmarks, exposed valleys and a highly seasonal mountain climate. The investigation connects territorial geography with the more intimate sequence of the pilgrimage route.

Landscape perception

Mapping where the journey gathers meaning.

A study of 1,729 geolocated social-media photographs was used to map concentrations of attention along the route. Five imageability elements—paths, edges, districts, nodes and landmarks—help identify the relationship between movement and perceived landscape.

The mappings inform the design questions and site selection; they are not evidence that any proposed intervention has been environmentally approved or constructed. Explore the separate research →

02 / Route strategy

Four responses within one landscape system.

Rather than placing identical structures at regular intervals, the proposal responds to different needs: religious interpretation, careful reuse, longer-duration accommodation and essential rest. Their locations were studied against visual-cultural mappings, hydrology, topography, existing facilities and environmental hazards.

03 / Interpretation

Keeping distance from the sacred ground.

The interpretation hall is conceived as a restrained setting for learning about the cultural and spiritual landscape near the sky burial platform. It stays away from the ritual ground: the studio proposal sets a separation of more than 300 metres from significant ritual locations, subject to further cultural and site-specific evaluation.

The ritual landscape

The landscape comes before the building.

The proposed hall creates a separate place for orientation and interpretation. Sacred ground remains untouched by the visitor facilities; the experience begins with the mountain, the route and the significance of the ritual setting.

Architectural approach

A building drawn into the terrain.

Partially embedded spaces reduce the hall's visual presence. Exhibition, film and observation are arranged as a continuous sequence, with timber, stone and rammed earth proposed as a restrained material palette.

Interior experience

Two ways of looking outward.

An interpretive gallery and a terrain display allow visitors to engage with the cultural landscape while retaining a direct visual connection with the mountain beyond.

04 / Adaptive reuse

Extending an existing place of arrival.

At Dirapuk Monastery, the proposal works with an established religious complex rather than creating a freestanding destination. Limited additions and the adaptation of an existing service structure establish a sequence of orientation, exhibition, rest and distant views.

Design intention

A threshold, not another landmark.

Working alongside the existing monastery, the proposal adds a modest entrance gallery and reuses a service structure for small-scale visitor functions. The new spaces remain subordinate to the established religious complex.

Architectural plan

Connecting existing and new.

The plan clarifies the relationship between monastery, administration, exhibition, viewing space and café, organizing them around an uninterrupted route of arrival.

Spatial experience

A sheltered pause along the route.

The semi-open interior offers rest, orientation and a distant view of the landscape without turning the monastery into an architectural backdrop.

Any intervention at the monastery would require the agreement and involvement of the relevant religious custodians.

05 / Accommodation

Renewing shelter within an existing footprint.

The First Tavern proposal reworks an existing pilgrim inn. Shared spaces, accommodation and circulation are reorganized around a protected courtyard, using renovation instead of a larger new building to support longer stays.

Adaptive accommodation

The courtyard becomes a point of return.

Existing accommodation and common facilities are reorganized around a sheltered courtyard rather than extending the building's footprint. A relief of the pilgrimage circuit turns the shared ground into a place to read and discuss the journey.

Stone, earth and timber maintain continuity with the surrounding built landscape.

Plans and room types

Renovation within an existing footprint.

Insulated rooms, circulation and shared services support longer stays. The drawings show how daily accommodation is arranged around the protected central space.

06 / Modular infrastructure

Shelter as a kit of parts.

Exposed stretches of the kora need essential services without the physical or operational footprint of a conventional hotel. The proposal develops prefabricated timber–aluminium units that can combine sleeping, dining, sanitation and service functions according to different stopping conditions.

Distributed support

Rest, replenish, continue.

Instead of concentrating services in one large building, repeatable modules can accommodate different stopping conditions along the route. Elevated supports, insulated enclosures and prefabricated components reduce the need for extensive site work.

Modular organization

Different uses, one construction logic.

Sleeping, dining and service rooms can be assembled in different combinations according to local capacity and seasonal needs.

Everyday occupation

From a bed to a shared table.

Two room types demonstrate how compact overnight accommodation and collective dining can be housed in the same modular system.

Construction and servicing

From fabrication to deployment.

Material preparation, prefabrication, transportation, assembly and replication are studied as one process. Delivery capacity, foundations, water, energy and maintenance would all need further site-specific verification.

Building section

Layers designed for maintenance.

The section distinguishes photovoltaic roofing, aluminium framing, insulation, prefabricated timber enclosure and raised foundations, making the proposed construction logic legible at a closer scale.

Scope & development

This project is an academic landscape architecture and facility-planning proposal, not an approved or completed intervention. Its diagrams and renders test relationships between site selection, ritual landscape, adaptive reuse, distributed shelter and construction logistics. Further development would require the involvement of religious custodians and local communities, as well as structural, ecological, hazard, service and operational assessment.
