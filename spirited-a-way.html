Spirited A Way

Mount KailashArchitecture & landscape2024

How much architecture does a sacred landscape need?

Design approach

Spirited A Way proposes three forms of support along the Mount Kailash pilgrimage landscape: a courtyard lodge, a compact supply station and an interpretation hall set away from the ritual ground. They address different durations and needs of occupation. The design uses siting, thresholds and restrained material presence to differentiate shelter, replenishment and cultural interpretation, rather than repeating one building type along the route.

01

The route determines the intervention.

The route map links the three intervention types to their intended roles. The lodge supports a longer stay, the station addresses a shorter pause and replenishment, and the hall offers a separate setting for interpretation. The distribution is a design response to the research overlays; it is not a record of built or approved facilities.

Each intervention has a different relationship to approach and occupation. The lodge gathers rooms around a protected shared interior; the supply point is compact and legible from the route; the hall maintains distance from the ritual setting while framing a sheltered encounter with the landscape. Their programme and scale should be read together with the movements and services needed to support them.

A

A courtyard for the first night.

The courtyard lodge works with the existing inn’s footprint. A new frame and insulated enclosure organise rooms around a sheltered common space, while the courtyard maintains an orientation toward the mountain’s north face. The design uses a known place of rest as its starting point rather than introducing an entirely separate destination.

The relationship between the room, covered threshold and courtyard offers different degrees of exposure. The shared interior supports rest and exchange after a demanding journey. Detailed thermal design, water supply, waste management and seasonal servicing would be developed with the people responsible for the facility.

B

A compact infrastructure of care.

The supply-station study combines compact rest and sanitation in prefabricated timber–aluminium modules. Elevated piles and an insulated enclosure are proposed to limit continuous ground contact and support a controlled interior within an exposed setting.

The compact plan concentrates functions instead of expanding them into a larger lodge. Its viability depends on delivery, servicing and waste removal as much as assembly. The project presents the module as a design hypothesis; logistics, environmental systems and site-specific foundation performance require further testing.

C

Keeping the horizon intact.

The interpretation hall is placed away from the ritual ground rather than occupying it as a viewing platform. Stone, rammed earth and timber form the proposed material palette. Enclosure and openings organise a sequence between the landscape, prayer square and sheltered interior.

The design seeks a measured presence, but material restraint alone does not establish cultural or ecological suitability. The siting and programme would need to be reconsidered with custodians and users, alongside specialist assessment of hazards, servicing and maintenance. The architectural drawings show the proposed response and the questions it still has to satisfy.

Scope & development

Research establishes the criteria for whether, where and how much to build. The project presents the particular lodge, station and hall designed to test those criteria; no cultural acceptance or environmental performance is inferred from the renderings.
