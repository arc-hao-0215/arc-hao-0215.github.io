Opening proposition

Water resilience is a relationship between protective systems, shared space and the institutions that keep both working.

Seventeen days of research in the Netherlands, within my SOM Foundation Fellowship, became a study of connections across scales: between a dwelling and its mooring, a street and its drainage, a public square and its storage capacity, a polder and its operating institutions. The journey is documented in Living with Water, an interactive field atlas. This essay draws out the architectural argument that links its individual places.

Research question

What changes when water becomes a condition around which everyday urban life is organised?

Defence and coexistence belong to the same system.

The familiar account of Dutch water management begins with the ability to exclude water. The field enquiry broadens the frame to include control, storage, accommodation and everyday occupation. These are overlapping strategies, not a simple historical progression in which one supersedes another. A floating neighbourhood still depends on regulated water levels, utilities and territorial protection.

The question for architecture is consequently not which single technology represents the future. It is how different capacities are connected, who can rely on them and what happens when one part is under stress. A basin that stores rainfall and a jetty that carries services are small spatial interfaces within much larger agreements.

Infrastructure extends beyond the visible object.

Kinderdijk, its pumping landscape and the Maeslantkering make very different forms of infrastructure visible. Read together, they shift attention from a recognisable object toward levels, connections, maintenance and coordinated operation. The atlas includes the Wisboomgemaal and Parksluizen because smaller operating components matter alongside celebrated structures.

A landscape can appear calm precisely because it is actively managed. The architectural lesson is to ask what makes that apparent stability possible. Drawings should record supply, servicing and responsibility as carefully as they record a building’s silhouette. The field account does not imply that every operating sequence was witnessed or that a visit can establish long-term performance.

The canal is an urban section.

At Utrecht’s Oudegracht, water, lower wharf, cellar, street and building form an unusually legible vertical arrangement. The municipality’s historical reconstructions show how tunnels and wharf cellars developed over time. The edge is therefore an evolving connection between movement, storage and occupation, rather than a single boundary drawn on a plan. [N1]

Amsterdam’s Canal Ring and Delft’s waterways extend the enquiry to the scale of the city. For design, the transferable question is how a water system establishes addresses and thresholds: where a door meets a quay, how a bridge reconnects streets, or how servicing can coexist with a place to linger. The section makes these relationships available for comparison.

A neighbourhood needs more than buoyancy.

Waterbuurt West is useful because it turns flotation into a problem of urban organisation. Rohmer’s project documentation describes shared jetties, flexible services, access and the separation of cars from the waterside circulation system. What appears as an individual floating house is supported by a collective framework. [N2]

Schoonschip adds a resident-led development model and shared infrastructure to this reading. Its jetty connects homes while organising services and social contact. The comparison shifts the question from whether a building can float to whether the surrounding system can support affordable, accessible and maintainable everyday life. [N3]

Storage can become a public room.

At Benthemplein, De Urbanisten combine stormwater storage with public space. Different basin depths and visible runoff routes make changing conditions part of the square’s spatial organisation. The project was shaped through workshops with neighbouring users, placing participation alongside the hydraulic design. [N4]

This produces a precise design question: can the same space remain useful under more than one environmental condition? Its public value cannot be judged only when the basins are empty, nor its hydraulic role only during a storm. The design must make transitions understandable and support care between events. That question also returns to the paired seasonal sections of the Jakarta proposal.

Institutions are part of the field.

The atlas includes exchanges at MVRDV, PosadMaxwan and TU Delft. Their relevance is the opportunity to consider how spatial ideas move among research, professional practice, public decisions and delivery. The visits sit alongside built cases rather than serving as a list of prestigious destinations.

Specific statements are not attributed to participants without a recorded basis. Instead, the enquiry carries a common question across these settings: what forms of evidence allow an architectural proposition to become a decision that institutions can implement and sustain? This extends water research from physical form to the organisation of knowledge.

Design for a capacity to change.

The strongest lesson is not that Dutch technologies can be copied into every flood-prone city. Land tenure, finance, governance and hydrology differ. Transfer should begin with a question about relationships: how to preserve access, share services, retain public space and organise responsibility while environmental conditions change.

For my design work, this means beginning with water and collective systems before treating the building as an autonomous object. A resilient spatial framework should make room for maintenance, uncertain conditions and different forms of occupation. The atlas remains the place to examine individual cases, published drawings and the distinction between field records and additional research sites.

Design the relationships that allow a place to keep working as water conditions change.
