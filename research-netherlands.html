Who Keeps the Water Out?

Water Management as a Spatial and Institutional Contract

Research / Field enquiry

A seemingly stable waterscape is often an actively managed one.

In the Netherlands, canals, waterfronts and floating neighbourhoods are frequently presented as evidence of a distinctive relationship with water. Yet their everyday stability depends on an extensive network of pumps, sluices, protective landscapes, access routes and operating institutions.

Undertaken during the SOM Foundation Fellowship in September 2026, this field enquiry follows those relationships across scales: from regional hydraulic systems to streets, public spaces and inhabited waterways. Photographs document what was observed in the field; conversations and published sources help describe the systems that cannot be understood through images alone.

Evidence keyFIELD Direct spatial observation & photographyEXCHANGE Informal on-site conversationsREFERENCE Published or institutional documentation

Research question

How do water-management systems shape the spatial organisation, everyday occupation and collective governance of Dutch cities?

In brief / What the evidence shows
01

Flood protection is an operating landscape

Water defence is sustained by monitored machinery, managed territory, maintenance and institutional readiness—not only by visible barriers.

Evidence: Field observations; Dutch Water Defence Lines [N5] and Rijkswaterstaat [N7]

02

Water management shapes urban sections

Wharves, below-grade bicycle parking and rainwater plazas show water-related systems reorganising levels of access and ordinary public use.

Evidence: Utrecht [N1], Stationsplein [N6], Benthemplein [N4]

03

Floating habitation relies on shared systems

Jetties, utility connections and forms of collective organisation are integral to water-based housing, though images alone cannot establish how governance performs.

Evidence: Field record and published project accounts [N2] [N3]

How to read the evidence

Read infrastructure at three scales

Field observation / spatial record ↗

The field record describes encounters with canals, floating neighbourhoods, public squares and water-control sites. These observations show spatial conditions, not the full history or performance of each system.

Documentary / archival evidence ↗

Published institutional material and site records explain hydraulic operations, governance and maintenance that cannot be inferred from a photograph alone.

Interpretation / limits ↗

The essay connects territorial defence, urban section and everyday occupation. This cross-scale reading is a synthesis; it is not a comparative engineering assessment.

These labels describe the role and limits of evidence in this essay. A photograph, published account and analytical diagram are not interchangeable forms of proof. Original source details remain in the captions and references below.

01 / Water as territoryFIELD ENQUIRY · SEP 2026

A landscape with two states

The Dutch Water Defence Lines reveal a historical relationship with water that goes beyond keeping it out. By deliberately inundating selected low-lying land, connected systems of sluices, dikes, canals and fortifications could temporarily transform agricultural territory into a defensive landscape. The system's operation depended on coordinated water levels rather than on the fortifications alone. [N5]

At Fort bij Vechten and the Waterliniemuseum, maps, interpretive installations and conversations made this largely territorial system more legible. What looks like an ordinary green landscape can carry an additional hydraulic history. The photographs document the educational encounter and surviving landscape markers; they are not evidence that historic inundation operations were observed.

02 / Water as infrastructureFIELD ENQUIRY · SEP 2026

The labour behind a stable landscape

At Kinderdijk, mills, pumping systems, waterways and dikes belong to a much larger history of polder drainage. Their relationship is not immediately apparent from any single building. The water level visible in a canal is the temporary outcome of equipment, maintenance and coordinated management.

The rotating equipment recorded during the trip provides a close view of mechanical water movement. At Parksluizen in Rotterdam, the lock, signal lights and control-room instruments connect that broader question to daily navigation. The control room photograph is particularly valuable: it makes the human operation behind an apparently fixed piece of infrastructure visible.

These are observations of physical components and operating environments, not measurements of pumping capacity, closure frequency or hydraulic performance. Such questions require technical and operational records.

Field case / Maeslantkering · Rijkswaterstaat

A movable boundary against the North Sea

Structure, operational readiness and the institutions responsible for maintaining flood protection.

At the Maeslantkering near Hoek van Holland, two vast movable steel gates form a storm-surge barrier protecting the Rotterdam region. The scale of this infrastructure is visible even in its ordinary, open condition: its trusses, supporting structures and operational grounds are built for an exceptional event rather than continuous closure. Rijkswaterstaat documents an automatic closure system supported by monitoring, testing, inspection and maintenance. [N7]

The field visit brought physical structure and institutional responsibility into a single encounter. A close-up photograph records the barrier's steelwork, while an on-site exchange with Rijkswaterstaat personnel situates that structure within a continuing practice of operational readiness. Neither image documents an actual storm closure, and the conversation is understood as qualitative field evidence rather than a measurement of hydraulic performance.

03 / Water as urban sectionFIELD ENQUIRY · SEP 2026

Between water, street and building

In Amsterdam's canal network, the waterway is a recurring urban address: bridges, building fronts, quays and public routes are arranged in relation to it. In Utrecht's Oudegracht, this relationship is especially visible in section. Lower wharves and cellars sit beneath upper-level streets, supporting a long history of changing urban uses. [N1]

At Amsterdam Centraal, Fietsenstalling Stationsplein provides another reading of the urban section. Bicycle access descends from the public street toward a parking hall beneath the Open Havenfront, with waterfront structures and everyday mobility occupying different levels of the same ground. The exterior and interior field photographs are read together; published project documentation establishes the facility’s location beneath the water. [N6]

The Albert Cuypgarage, separately documented beneath Amsterdam’s Boerenwetering canal, offers a related published example of transport infrastructure sharing space with an urban waterway.

Field case / Haagse Veer / De Schuine Lijn

Residential terraces at the water's edge

At De Schuine Lijn along Haagse Veer and Sint Jacobsplaats, late-twentieth-century housing presents a different urban section from Utrecht's historic wharves. Stepped and inclined residential forms meet the water through terraces, balconies and waterside pedestrian routes. Built as part of an inner-city densification programme between 1975 and 1982, the ensemble combined housing with commercial premises and walking connections. The photograph records these visible interfaces rather than attributing an unverified flood-management function to the adjoining water. [N9]

04 / Water as public spaceFIELD ENQUIRY · SEP 2026

The square in its dry state

Benthemplein in Rotterdam was observed in its ordinary, dry-weather condition. The sunken surfaces, retaining edges and stepped seating are legible as places for movement and gathering. Their second function—as temporary rainwater storage—comes from the documented hydraulic connections to the surrounding catchment. [N4]

The photograph does not show stormwater retention taking place. Instead, it records the crucial interval between rainfall events, when a hydraulic space participates in everyday public life. Read beside published project information, the image makes two temporal conditions visible without confusing observed use with claimed storage performance.

Field case / ZOHO / Regentuin

A rainwater store embedded in the neighbourhood

The ZOHO Rain Letters work at a smaller, distributed scale than Benthemplein. The timber letters identify the Zomerhofkwartier while also storing rainwater collected from the nearby Hofbogen roof. According to the City of Rotterdam and the project team, a weather-responsive system releases stored water into the surrounding rain garden ahead of forecast heavy rain, while retaining moisture for planting during dry periods. The field photograph captures the installation's public presence; its internal controls and water-storage operation are understood through published documentation, not direct measurement. [N8]

Field case / RiF010 / Steigersgracht

An urban canal used for surfing

At RiF010, part of Rotterdam's Steigersgracht has acquired a recreational role through a mechanically generated surf wave. The facility opened in July 2024 and brings water sports into close proximity with residential buildings, streets and pedestrian quays. Controlled waves make this urban waterway visibly active, while the adjoining edges accommodate spectators and passersby. Unlike Benthemplein or the ZOHO rain garden, RiF010 is primarily a recreational installation rather than stormwater-retention infrastructure. It documents another relationship between managed water and everyday public life. [N10]

Field observation / Public life

Visible in the photograph: the artificial wave, the surfer, the canal wall and a pedestrian embankment directly beside the city's buildings. Water is simultaneously a managed facility, an activity space and a spectacle in the urban landscape.

05 / Living on waterFIELD ENQUIRY · SEP 2026

The collective infrastructure of floating neighbourhoods

Floating habitation depends on far more than buoyancy. In Waterbuurt West, published project documentation describes shared jetties, flexible service connections and an organisation that separates waterside movement from road traffic. [N2]

In Schoonschip, the field photographs record dwellings separated by water, narrow access decks, vegetation, small domestic platforms and everyday objects at the edge. These details describe how a neighbourhood is occupied, not only how its buildings float. Resident-led organisation and collective services provide an important institutional context to the spatial observations. [N3]

Informal exchanges during the visit also foregrounded water as a setting for swimming, boating and contact with nature. Such conversations contribute qualitative field impressions, but do not constitute a representative survey of residents.

Field encounter / Waterbuurt West, IJburg

Everyday knowledge of floating habitation

An informal on-site conversation at Waterbuurt West in IJburg added a human perspective to the observation of floating housing designed by Architectenbureau Marlies Rohmer. From the shared pedestrian jetty, bicycles, planting, access decks and the close proximity of individual dwellings reveal how this planned water neighbourhood is used in everyday life. The exchange is part of the qualitative field record, not a systematic interview or a representative assessment of residents' views. [N2]

06 / The public waterfrontFIELD ENQUIRY · SEP 2026

Between urban development and everyday access

Along the Nieuwe Maas, Rotterdam's river forms a continuous foreground to metropolitan development, transport infrastructure and prominent waterfront buildings. Seen from across the water, the Erasmusbrug and the opposing banks establish the river's role in organising urban form. The panorama records this relationship at a distance; the accessibility of individual waterfront spaces still requires observation at ground level.

A waterfront is not automatically a public place. Its accessibility depends on paths, thresholds, gradients, opening arrangements and the distribution of private and shared space. At Tuschinskipark in Amsterdam, a planted walking route mediates between housing and the water. At other photographed sites, the edge takes different forms: occupied quays, mooring spaces and passages between buildings and the waterfront.

Floating Office Rotterdam and Amsterdam's Sluishuis illustrate two ways contemporary buildings relate to a wider waterside setting. Their architectural form is visible in the photographs, but their degree of public accessibility must be assessed using on-site routes and management information, not inferred from an attractive view alone.

These observations shift the focus from the waterfront as scenery toward the conditions under which people can reach, cross, occupy or simply look toward the water.

07 / People and institutionsFIELD ENQUIRY · SEP 2026

The systems behind the systems

Evidence boundary. Site visits show the physical presence of systems and reveal questions about responsibility. Routine monitoring, gate operation and maintenance outcomes are described from institutional documentation; they were not independently audited during the field enquiry.

Photographs make structures visible; they rarely reveal the full organisation required to keep them working. The field enquiry included encounters with people involved in interpreting, operating, researching and inhabiting water-related environments. Recorded visits and informal discussions at the Waterliniemuseum, Kinderdijk, Parksluizen, Maeslantkering and residential sites raised questions that cannot be settled by a site image.

Who maintains a shared jetty? Who authorises a lock operation? How are hydraulic landscapes explained after their original functions have changed? Who is responsible for access when weather, water levels or servicing interrupt ordinary routines?

The study distinguishes these research questions from established findings. Published documentation supports some descriptions of project organisation; interviews were not undertaken as a systematic institutional survey. Further evidence is needed before making claims about long-term cost, reliability or governance effectiveness.

08 / Research findingsFIELD ENQUIRY · SEP 2026

A collective condition, not a single solution

Across these Dutch sites, several relationships recur. Contemporary recreational water spaces such as RiF010 extend the enquiry beyond protection and accommodation to organised leisure and public activity. Protection and accommodation coexist: an inhabited water edge remains linked to territorial systems of control. Infrastructure operates across scales: a jetty, canal or public basin cannot be understood without the larger networks around it. Water shapes ordinary urban life: buildings, streets, wharves and public spaces take their form and use partly from water. Management is inseparable from space: maintenance, responsibilities and access remain crucial even where they are difficult to photograph.

The study does not identify a universally applicable Dutch model. It documents specific sites and the interdependencies observed or described there. What appears to be a single water-related landscape is often the work of multiple technical systems, social practices and public institutions operating together.

A waterscape is not maintained by its form alone.
