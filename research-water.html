Opening proposition

Adaptation already exists before the architect arrives. Infrastructure must learn from the practices that sustain everyday life.

Flood resilience begins with the routines that keep a neighbourhood functioning. Raising a floor, moving possessions, sharing shelter and maintaining access are spatial practices as well as acts of care. The research translates a reading of these practices into a framework that can change with the river.

Research question

How can civic infrastructure learn from the everyday adaptations already practised in Kampung Melayu, allowing access, livelihood, care and collective life to continue as water levels change?

Three scales of an uneven water system.

At the metropolitan scale, the research notes connect flooding with land subsidence, groundwater dependence, urbanisation and drainage. At the river scale, the Ciliwung brings questions of water quality, settlement and access into contact. At the neighbourhood scale, Kampung Melayu exposes how a change in water level affects housing, work, sanitation and routes to shared services.

This macro–meso–micro reading prevents a single technical response from standing in for the whole problem. Protecting a building does not automatically protect its inhabitants’ livelihoods or their access to care. The framework therefore reads hydrological exposure together with social continuity.

Adaptation is already spatial.

Research on informal adaptation in North Jakarta directs attention to the everyday knowledge and practices through which communities respond to flooding. This study uses that literature as a comparative lens for raised living spaces, protected storage, temporary access and mutual assistance. These are literature-informed design questions; they are not the results of a new household survey in Kampung Melayu. [J5] [J6]

The notes draw from different Jakarta contexts, including North Jakarta. Their transfer to Kampung Melayu must therefore remain a question for site-specific investigation. The architectural inference is to support existing capacities while addressing the points where individual adaptation cannot maintain a collective service.

Adaptation is not the return to a fixed normal. It is the capacity for everyday life to continue through change.

A stable datum, changing occupation.

Paired dry- and flood-season sections are the principal design test. The railway viaduct is read as a relatively stable spatial datum around which access and occupation can be reorganised. Keeping the frame constant makes it possible to compare what changes: thresholds, movement, storage and the relationship between public rooms and water.

The dry-season section accommodates markets, workshops, kitchens and shared space. Flood conditions shift parts of the system toward refuge, boat landings and relief. The finding is a design inference: continuity may depend on a durable support with adaptable use, rather than a return to an identical ground-level arrangement after every flood.

Water security extends beyond the flood event.

The proposal links roof-water collection, wastewater treatment and wetland buffers. This extends the study from flood avoidance to the relationship between clean supply, contaminated flows and the public landscape. Storage, treatment and overflow should be legible as distinct parts of a cycle, not collapsed into a claim that all water can be safely reused.

The spatial consequence is to place environmental infrastructure alongside daily civic use. Wetland edges and modular dwellings are studied as connected components of a river corridor; the section exposes their relationships while leaving treatment capacity and engineering performance open to verification.

A framework to test with residents.

The 2025 application proposal and itinerary describe intended comparative research. They are not evidence that every listed visit, interview or activity was completed. The later LinkedIn profile records international field research, but the supplied documents do not include a complete field log or interview dataset. No first-person resident testimony is invented here.

The Jakarta proposal establishes questions that continue in the later Netherlands field research: how access, shared infrastructure and water management can support ordinary life through change. The Netherlands enquiry and its linked field atlas develop that comparison without treating Dutch projects as ready-made solutions for Jakarta.
