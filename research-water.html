Research / Enquiry 05

When the River Occupies the Street

Everyday flood adaptation & collective life along Jakarta’s Ciliwung River

Opening proposition

When the Ciliwung River overflows into Kampung Melayu, a street can temporarily cease to function as a street. Ground floors become inundated, belongings move upstairs and daily life is reorganised around the spaces that remain accessible.

For residents of this riverside community in East Jakarta, flooding is not only an exceptional event. Repeated exposure has shaped how people occupy their homes, protect possessions, share resources and respond collectively to disruption. These adaptations are often modest, improvised and absent from conventional infrastructure plans. Yet they reveal a crucial dimension of urban resilience: the work of sustaining everyday life where water cannot always be excluded.

This enquiry examines documented flood-adaptation practices in Kampung Melayu—not an architectural proposal—alongside the uneven burdens of recovery and the consequences of flood-control policies that treat relocation as a solution.

Research question

How do residents of flood-prone urban communities adapt their homes, routines and shared spaces to recurring inundation, and what do these practices reveal about water, infrastructure and collective life?

Research approach

The enquiry reads Jakarta across three scales: metropolitan water conditions, the Ciliwung river corridor and the lived fabric of Kampung Melayu. Spatial observations and mapping are considered alongside published research on residents’ responses, shelter provision, relocation and post-flood recovery.

The most detailed household accounts here come from Rahmayati, Parnell and Himmayani’s fieldwork in Kebon Pala and Tanah Rendah, published in 2017. Studies by Prana and colleagues concern North Jakarta and are used as comparisons, not as direct evidence about Kampung Melayu. The discussion also draws on documented March 2025 flooding. Reported resident behaviour is attributed to these sources; it is not presented as a new household interview survey. [J1] [J2]

01 / Territory

A city built with water

Jakarta occupies a low-lying landscape shaped by rivers, seasonal rainfall and the movement of water between upstream catchments and the coast. Urban growth, hard surfaces, altered drainage and groundwater use have transformed these relationships. Flooding, however, does not have one cause or one geography: river overflow in East Jakarta differs from the combination of tidal exposure and subsidence affecting parts of the northern coast.

The Ciliwung River carries upstream conditions through densely inhabited districts. In Kampung Melayu, that larger system is encountered at the scale of thresholds, lanes, water supply and household routines. A metropolitan inundation map can identify exposure; it cannot by itself explain whether a family can reach a safe route, use a toilet or protect its livelihood.

This difference of scale matters. Water is both a territorial system and an immediate change to the places through which daily life takes place. [J1] [J2]

02 / Domestic space

The house learns to flood

In Kebon Pala and Tanah Rendah, the 2017 study documented residents responding to lower levels of flooding by moving to the upper floors of predominantly two-storey homes. People lifted televisions, refrigerators, stoves, fans, food and other valuables away from the water, while moving vehicles and sometimes livestock to safer places. When conditions worsened, they evacuated to nearby higher ground. These are descriptions of households in that study, not fixed flood-depth instructions for others to follow. [J1]

These actions temporarily change the organisation of the dwelling. An upper storey becomes a protected living area and store; the stair becomes the connection between wet and dry space; the ground floor may cease to support its usual activities. A house is thus occupied differently according to the condition of the river, even without a formal change in its plan.

Other studies of flood-adapted kampungs in North Jakarta identify raised structures, locally assembled materials and changing uses of ground as part of a wider repertoire of living with water. Such practices illuminate possibilities for comparison, but they cannot simply be transferred to this riverine neighbourhood. [J2] [J3]

03 / Access

When movement changes level

In Kampung Melayu, lanes connect houses, small markets, schools and neighbourhood meeting places. The field study recorded differences in lane width and accessibility, including widened points used for community interaction and trade. When water enters these routes, distances measured in metres no longer tell the whole story of access. [J1]

A familiar passage can become difficult or unsafe, while an elevated road, accessible building or temporary landing gains unexpected importance. At a certain point, residents may need boats and trained emergency services to reach safety. In March 2025, a government report documented boat evacuations in Kampung Melayu after approximately 1.5 metres of water inundated homes in one affected area. [J5]

The street is therefore more than an engineered surface. It is a link between people and services whose continuity changes with river conditions. The neighbourhood can contain dry rooms yet remain difficult to inhabit if routes to them are interrupted.

04 / Shared institutions

The neighbourhood as refuge

Schools and small prayer spaces (mushollas) are part of Kampung Melayu’s ordinary social geography. During flooding, some have taken on temporary shelter functions. In the 2017 research, a musholla housed women and children but was limited by its size; a private primary school offered shelter to members of the organisation that operated it. Existing institutions provided refuge, while also revealing gaps in access, sanitation, privacy and capacity. [J1]

The same pattern remained visible in March 2025. Jakarta’s public reporting identified Kampung Melayu schools as evacuation centres and documented residents occupying classrooms. By 6 March, city officials reported that those sheltered in identified schools and a district-government facility had returned home, even as cleaning work continued. [J7] [J6]

Shelter is consequently not reducible to a building above flood level. People need drinking water, toilets, electricity, sufficient space, reliable distribution and ways to reach relatives and possessions. Familiarity and proximity are important, but so are the limitations of facilities never intended for prolonged displacement.

05 / Cooperation

Gotong royong

Gotong royong—mutual assistance and shared work—is one way of understanding the social arrangements that help neighbourhoods function during disruption. The Kampung Melayu study records residents’ involvement in recovery, with attention first to cleaning sediment and debris from shared infrastructure and services before repairs moved more fully into individual homes. Community leaders played an organising role. [J1]

These acts reveal an important kind of infrastructure that rarely appears as an object on a plan: relationships through which a lane is cleared, information is shared or a public facility is reopened. Yet cooperation is not unlimited capacity. Households have unequal time, equipment, income and ability to contribute labour.

Recognising community-led recovery should not mean treating residents as substitutes for public maintenance or emergency provision. Informal capacity and institutional responsibility exist together, and the limits of the first make the second necessary. [J3]

06 / Housing and governance

Staying is not the same as being safe

In the 2017 interviews, some residents expressed reluctance to leave their homes during floods unless conditions became severe. Protecting belongings was one reason; overcrowded, uncomfortable temporary shelters were another. The same study documented attachment to neighbourhood ties across generations. These findings describe the particular residents interviewed; they do not mean that all Kampung Melayu households make the same choice. [J1]

Permanent relocation poses a different question. As part of the Ciliwung normalisation programme in 2015, households in nearby Kampung Pulo were moved into the Jatinegara Barat rental apartment complex. A 2022 study examined the effects on those relocated and on people remaining near the river. It found that social and economic outcomes were mixed and differed between the two groups. [J4]

The preference to stay in a familiar place should not be mistaken for acceptance of unsafe conditions. Equally, reducing physical flood exposure does not automatically preserve access to work, social networks or household economic security.

What counts as protection depends on more than the position of a building relative to the river. It also depends on what residents stand to lose when they move.

07 / After inundation

Water after the flood

Flooding does not end when water retreats. Sediment and waste may remain in streets and rooms; electricity, sanitation, food storage and household equipment may still be unusable. The 2017 field study described recovery beginning with collective services and shared infrastructure, followed by clearing affected homes. This work occupies a period that is easy to overlook when analysis focuses only on the flood peak. [J1]

The March 2025 response makes that sequence tangible. On 4 March, emergency responders evacuated residents by boat from flooded homes in Kampung Melayu. On 6 March, local authorities reported that displaced residents had returned to their homes while public workers and other agencies continued post-flood cleaning. The return of occupants and the restoration of normal conditions were not necessarily the same event. [J5] [J6]

Recovery is not the simple reversal of inundation. It requires labour, functioning institutions and time, and those demands fall differently on different households.

08 / Inequality

The unequal capacity to adapt

The practices documented in Kampung Melayu contradict the idea that flood-prone residents merely wait for outside assistance. Households reorganise rooms; neighbours coordinate movement and cleaning; local institutions temporarily change use. The resulting knowledge is practical, accumulated and specific to place.

But the possibility of adapting is not shared equally. An accessible second storey, a safer route, disposable income, the ability to miss work and an existing support network all alter what a household can do. Public toilets, dependable drinking water and safe shelters cannot be created through household ingenuity alone. [J1]

Research in North Jakarta similarly calls attention to the conflict between local practices of living with water and formal planning approaches that may discount them. The comparison is illuminating, but differences between coastal and riverine flood conditions matter. [J2] [J3]

Resilience should therefore not become a flattering label for communities repeatedly forced to absorb preventable damage. Existing adaptations demonstrate capacity, but also show where individual and collective resources are being stretched beyond reasonable limits.

09 / Findings

Living with an unsettled river

The investigation brings together four overlapping dimensions: domestic adjustment, changing neighbourhood access, collective shelter and recovery, and the institutions governing infrastructure and relocation. None can be understood through a single flood-control structure or architectural type.

Much of the detailed behavioural evidence comes from site-specific fieldwork published in 2017. The documented March 2025 event shows continued exposure and the use of public institutions, but does not establish that all earlier practices remain unchanged. Current household surveys and more extensive seasonal observation would be needed to test the full picture today.

When the river occupies the street, the city does not simply stop functioning. Its inhabitants reorganise everyday life around the spaces and relationships that remain. Understanding those practices also means recognising what residents cannot sustain alone.

The central finding is not an argument for a particular building or a promise that informal adaptation is sufficient. It is a way of seeing flood-prone urban life as a set of material, social and institutional relationships continually renegotiated through water.
