Opening proposition

The words used to name a territory can reveal relationships that land-use categories leave out.

Two glossary entries approach Taiwan’s rurban territories through language. 農地種工廠 names an incremental process of industrialisation on farmland. Niyaro’ describes community through collective territory and relations rather than rural location alone. Read together, the terms ask how architecture can respond to mixed territories without reducing them to an incomplete version of either city or countryside.

Related programme and research context

Research question

What do local terms make visible where the city and countryside coexist?

Naming the rurban interface.

The Glossary of Rurbanity forms part of Harvard GSD’s Carrots and Sticks: Incentives Shaping the “Rurban” Interface conference, held on 11 September 2026. The call brings keywords, short essays and images into a comparative discussion of how urban and rural conditions are named in different languages. [R1]

The value of a glossary is not simply translation. A word can indicate what must be explained: a process, a shared territory, a mode of production or an institutional mismatch. These entries move from meaning to spatial condition and then to a design question.

農地種工廠 — Planting Factories on Farmland.

Nóngdì zhòng gōngchǎng names a process rather than just a land-use type. Factories are “planted” parcel by parcel while fields, irrigation channels, rural roads and settlements remain legible. Wu’s research distinguishes this extended urbanisation from an earlier condition in which rural industry drew on a countryside labour pool: peripheral farmland becomes a land pool as production moves outward. [R2]

Across the Taichung–Changhua belt, networks of small firms and subcontractors meet agricultural parcels and uneven regulation. Buck examines the construction and decentralisation of Taiwan’s industrial networks; Lin and Su’s Dingfanpo study brings the environmental and distributive conflicts of agricultural-industrial coexistence into focus. [R3] [R4]

A design field between parcels.

Regulatory responses include pathways for eligible older unregistered factories under Taiwan’s Factory Management and Counseling Act. Changhua’s June 2026 planning review addresses designated specific-factory zones. These mechanisms organise registration and land-use review; they do not by themselves resolve every spatial relationship within a mixed territory. [R5] [R6]

The unresolved design field lies between parcels: irrigation and drainage, road access, fire safety, pollution control and agricultural continuity. The question is how to retrofit these shared systems without making either the expulsion of industry or wholesale conversion of farmland the only imaginable response.

Niyaro’ — Community beyond a rural location.

Niyaro’ is a Pangcah / ’Amis word for a village or community; it does not mean “peri-urban.” Huang’s account and Wu’s research on Xizhou help situate community through collective territory, kinship, work, ritual, shared space and self-organisation. Urban riverbank communities demonstrate why these relations cannot be reduced to distance from a metropolitan centre. [R7] [R8]

At Xizhou, on the Xindian River, cultivation, gathering and ritual life accompanied self-built housing. Flood-control and demolition pressure led to negotiation over a safer form of staying. The municipal account records the nearby rehousing of 34 households through a “333” self-help funding model and shared cultural facilities. [R9]

What must move with a house?

Relocation is more than moving dwelling units. If community is sustained through livelihood, ecology, collective space and ritual, then retaining a building count is an incomplete measure of continuity. The design question is what must move with the houses for a niyaro’ to remain a niyaro’.

This entry is a reading of published scholarship and public project documentation, not a claim to speak for Xizhou residents. Questions of cultural meaning and acceptable change require the community’s own authority. Architecture can make the spatial stakes visible without assuming that a plan settles them.

Two terms, different forms of continuity.

The two terms should not be collapsed into one generic category of informality. One traces industrialisation through farmland; the other names the continuing relations of an Indigenous community. Both expose a mismatch between a static spatial category and an active territorial process.

For design, the implication is to investigate the shared systems that sustain a place before prescribing a new identity for it. In one case those systems include irrigation, production and environmental burdens; in the other, collective space, cultural practice and the ability to remain. Language becomes an instrument for deciding what architecture must notice.

Before drawing a boundary, ask what relationships the local name already holds together.
