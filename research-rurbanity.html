Planting Factories, Moving Villages

Two terms for territories that resist the rural–urban divide

Opening proposition

A territory is never fully described by the category assigned to it.

Rurbanity is not simply the overlap of rural and urban forms. It exposes the limits of the terms through which land, production and community are classified, governed and understood. In Taiwan, 農地種工廠 describes the incremental occupation of farmland by industry. Niyaro’ directs attention towards the collective relations through which a community sustains itself beyond a fixed rural location.

These words arise from different histories and political conditions. Their comparison is not an equivalence: it is a way of asking what maps and administrative classifications make visible, and what they leave outside their frame.

Research question

What can local words tell us about territories that conventional land-use categories cannot describe?

00 / FrameworkLanguage · Territory · Governance

Naming the rurban interface

The value of a glossary is not simply translation. A local term can name a process, an inherited social relation or a contradiction that a standard rural–urban distinction misses. The Glossary of Rurbanity collects situated contributions in this spirit. [R1]

AMO’s Countryside research provides a useful comparative prompt to look beyond metropolitan categories, but it does not define either of these terms. The meanings of 農地種工廠 and Niyaro’ must be situated in their own scholarship, histories and communities. [R10]

00 / MethodFrom a word to a spatial condition

Follow the relations that a category cannot contain

Each entry begins with a term and follows four lines of enquiry: what it names; at what scale it operates; which material or social relations extend beyond that scale; and what disappears when the territory is represented as discrete land-use classes.

農地種工廠 / Production

Industrialisation through agricultural parcels, subcontracting networks, inherited rural roads and shared environmental infrastructures.

Niyaro’ / Collective life

Community understood through kinship, shared space, ritual, livelihood and relationships that exceed a fixed residential location.

The cases remain distinct. The political ecology of industrial production on farmland cannot be assimilated to the claims of an Indigenous community negotiating settlement and continuity.

01 / 農地種工廠Taichung–Changhua belt · Central Taiwan

Industrialisation without the erasure of agricultural land

農地種工廠 / Nóngdì zhòng gōngchǎngPlanting factories on farmland.

A process of territorial transformation, not merely a label for factories located on farmland. Production is inserted into individual plots while the fine-grained agricultural field pattern remains visible.

Wu (2019) distinguishes this extended urbanisation from an earlier countryside organised as a labour pool for manufacturing. As production moves outward, farmland becomes a land pool. Across the Taichung–Changhua industrial-agricultural belt, dispersed firms and subcontractors operate within a landscape shaped by agricultural parcels, rural roads and uneven land-use regulation. [R2] [R3]

Lin and Su’s study of plumbing-hardware manufacture in Dingfanpo, Lukang, examines how industrial and agricultural uses coexist while their environmental burdens are distributed unevenly. [R4]

01.1 / Field conditionPhotographic record

The countryside did not disappear. It became industrial.

The landscape of coexistence

Warehouses and workshops do not form a single separate industrial estate. They appear among working fields, houses and village streets.

The photograph makes a mixed territory visible before it is reduced to a diagram: agriculture and manufacturing occupy a common everyday landscape, though they do not necessarily share risks or benefits equally.

01.2 / Spatial patternParcel · Settlement · Infrastructure

Industrialisation by parcel, not by district

Factories are inserted into individual plots while the inherited agricultural grid continues to structure access and adjacency. The resulting pattern is neither a conventional industrial zone nor an intact rural landscape. It is an incremental industrialisation operating through the existing territorial grain.

Inherited parcel structure

Fine-grained field boundaries remain readable in the land-use mosaic.

Incremental occupation

Manufacturing and settlement are interspersed rather than collected in one continuous zone.

Shared territorial lines

Roads and drainage connect activities across separate property boundaries.

The map is a visual interpretation grounded in publicly available agricultural land-use material. It is not a verified cadastral register of each factory, land-use permit or contaminated site.

01.3 / Territorial readingFarming · Coexistence · Industry

The productive landscape exceeds the boundary of its parcels

A factory may occupy one piece of land, yet its operation depends on delivery routes, workers, water, drainage and subcontracting relations across a much larger area. Farming likewise relies on collective infrastructural continuity. The coexistence of these activities is therefore a question of the territory between parcels, not only what happens inside them.

The panoramic drawing is a conceptual synthesis of interconnected systems. Its labels identify lines of enquiry rather than measured territorial shares or a completed planning proposal.

01.4 / Institutional paradoxLand designation · Productive use

Same land. Different realities.

In law, land may remain designated for agriculture. In everyday use, factories, housing and logistics can already shape its operation. The upper and lower layers of the following diagram visualise that disjunction; they are not intended to certify the legal status of each illustrated building.

Land-use classification describes the status of a parcel. It cannot by itself describe the movement of water, goods or environmental burdens across property lines.

01.5 / Regulation and impactPolicy · Shared infrastructure

The territory is shared. Its governance is fragmented.

Taiwan’s Factory Management and Counseling Act establishes pathways for eligible older unregistered factories to improve safety and seek specific-factory registration and subsequent review. Changhua County’s June 2026 planning review similarly addresses specific-factory zoning in landscapes of agricultural-industrial mixing. [R5] [R6]

These are responses to a real administrative dilemma, but recognition at the level of the individual plot does not exhaust the relationships that connect adjacent plots. Drainage, road access, agricultural continuity, industrial emissions and exposure all call for analysis at more than one scale. The scholarship establishes the presence of political-ecological conflict; the diagrams here do not present new pollution sampling or an environmental impact assessment. [R4]

The boundary of ownership is not the boundary of impact.
02 / NIYARO’Xizhou · Xindian River

Community beyond a rural location

Niyaro’ is a Pangcah / ’Amis word for village or community; it should not be flattened into a translation of “peri-urban”. Scholarship by Huang and Wu helps situate Indigenous collective life through relations of kinship, livelihood, shared territory, ritual and self-organisation. These relationships cannot be measured simply by distance from the city. [R7] [R8]

In Xizhou on the Xindian River, self-built housing developed alongside shared cultivation, gathering and cultural practices. Flood-control and demolition pressures prompted negotiations over staying near the existing community. A municipal account documents the rehousing of 34 households through a “333” self-help model and shared facilities. [R9]

02.1 / Settlement and relationBeyond the dwelling unit

What continues when a settlement moves?

Relocation is not adequately understood through a count of replacement dwellings. Space for communal gathering, cultivation, livelihood and ritual can be as constitutive of place as the house itself. The continuity of these relations can neither be assumed from a new floor plan nor measured solely in the number of rehoused households.

This reading is based on published scholarship and public documentation. It is not a first-hand account of the residents’ experience, nor a claim to speak on behalf of the community. Interpretations of cultural continuity must remain accountable to community voices and decisions.

02.2 / Collective territorySpatial continuity · Self-determination

The inventory that a housing count misses

Material inventory

Buildings, access routes, housing area and public facilities are readily recorded by conventional project documentation.

Relational inventory

Kinship, communal spaces, everyday cultivation, ritual and proximity depend on social relations that cannot be represented by a building count alone.

What should be carried forward, transformed or left behind cannot be determined by an external architectural reading. The central research question concerns which relationships matter to residents themselves, and how those relationships are shaped by the realities of risk, governance and displacement.

03 / Comparative readingTwo terms · Different continuities

Two terms, different forms of continuity

農地種工廠 traces how an agricultural territory is industrialised through its existing parcel structure. Niyaro’ situates community in relations that persist beyond a narrowly defined settlement site. One concerns the spatial and political ecology of production; the other concerns Indigenous collective life, territory and self-determination.

They are not equivalent examples of “informality”. Read together, they reveal why static categories are inadequate when the processes that sustain a place cross the boundaries through which it is represented.

Before drawing a boundary, ask which relationships the local name already holds together.
