Where Factories Take Root

農地種工廠 — Industrialisation through the agricultural grid

Opening proposition

Farmland can become industrial without ceasing to look agricultural.

In central Taiwan, factories are planted among fields, village settlements, rural roads and irrigation channels. Instead of replacing an agricultural landscape in a single act of rezoning, manufacturing enters the inherited grid one parcel at a time. Agriculture remains physically legible even as the productive use of the land changes.

This essay examines 農地種工廠 — literally, “planting factories on farmland” — as a territorial process rather than a fixed land-use category. Through published research, a photographic record of Dingfanpo in Lukang and original spatial diagrams, it traces a disjunction among legal designation, everyday production and the infrastructures shared across property boundaries.

Research question

What happens when industry occupies farmland parcel by parcel, while the agricultural grid continues to organise the landscape?

01 / Term and conditionTaichung–Changhua · Central Taiwan

From agricultural labour pool to industrial land pool

農地種工廠 / Nóngdì zhòng gōngchǎngPlanting factories on farmland.

A process of territorial industrialisation, not simply the presence of a factory on agricultural land. Factories are inserted into individual plots, while fields, roads, irrigation and settlements continue to organise the territory.

Wu (2019) uses the term to distinguish a later form of extended urbanisation from an earlier industrial countryside structured by a rural labour pool. As manufacturing decentralises, peripheral agricultural land becomes a land pool for dispersed industrial production. The geography of industry expands, but it does so through the existing agricultural parcel structure rather than through an entirely new industrial district. [R2]

Within the Taichung–Changhua belt, networks of small firms and subcontractors intersect with land costs, uneven land-use regulation and established rural infrastructure. Buck (2000) describes the decentralisation of Taiwan’s industrial networks, while Lin and Su’s (2023) study of plumbing-hardware manufacture in Dingfanpo traces the political ecology of agricultural and industrial coexistence. [R3] [R4]

Research approach. The following sequence moves from a photographic landscape to parcel patterns, shared infrastructures and regulatory categories. Maps and conceptual diagrams synthesise spatial relationships; they are not a parcel-by-parcel legal audit or new environmental sampling.

02 / Landscape observationPhotographic record

The countryside did not disappear. It became industrial.

The landscape of coexistence

Warehouses and workshops do not form a single separate industrial estate. They appear among working fields, houses and village streets.

The photograph makes a mixed territory visible before it is reduced to a diagram: agriculture and manufacturing occupy a common everyday landscape, though they do not necessarily share risks or benefits equally.

03 / Spatial patternParcel · Settlement · Infrastructure

Industrialisation by parcel, not by district

Factories are inserted into individual plots while the inherited agricultural grid continues to structure access and adjacency. The resulting pattern is neither a conventional industrial zone nor an intact rural landscape. It is an incremental industrialisation operating through the existing territorial grain.

Inherited parcel structure

Fine-grained field boundaries remain readable in the land-use mosaic.

Incremental occupation

Manufacturing and settlement are interspersed rather than collected in one continuous zone.

Shared territorial lines

Roads and drainage connect activities across separate property boundaries.

The map is a visual interpretation grounded in publicly available agricultural land-use material. It is not a verified cadastral register of each factory, land-use permit or contaminated site.

04 / Territorial systemsFarming · Coexistence · Industry

The productive landscape exceeds the boundary of its parcels

A factory may occupy one piece of land, yet its operation depends on delivery routes, workers, water, drainage and subcontracting relations across a much larger area. Farming likewise relies on collective infrastructural continuity. The coexistence of these activities is therefore a question of the territory between parcels, not only what happens inside them.

The panoramic drawing is a conceptual synthesis of interconnected systems. Its labels identify lines of enquiry rather than measured territorial shares or a completed planning proposal.

05 / Institutional paradoxLand designation · Productive use

Same land. Different realities.

In law, land may remain designated for agriculture. In everyday use, factories, housing and logistics can already shape its operation. The upper and lower layers of the following diagram visualise that disjunction; they are not intended to certify the legal status of each illustrated building.

Land-use classification describes the status of a parcel. It cannot by itself describe the movement of water, goods or environmental burdens across property lines.

06 / Regulation and impactPolicy · Shared infrastructure

The territory is shared. Its governance is fragmented.

Taiwan’s Factory Management and Counseling Act establishes pathways for eligible older unregistered factories to improve safety and seek specific-factory registration and subsequent review. Changhua County’s June 2026 planning review similarly addresses specific-factory zoning in landscapes of agricultural-industrial mixing. [R5] [R6]

These are responses to a real administrative dilemma, but recognition at the level of the individual plot does not exhaust the relationships that connect adjacent plots. Drainage, road access, agricultural continuity, industrial emissions and exposure all call for analysis at more than one scale. The scholarship establishes the presence of political-ecological conflict; the diagrams here do not present new pollution sampling or an environmental impact assessment. [R4]

07 / Research findingsParcel · Territory · Governance

The agricultural grid persists. The rules governing it do not fully describe what happens within it.

農地種工廠 reveals a form of industrialisation that does not depend on the complete physical disappearance of farmland. The territorial lines inherited from agricultural production remain visible, yet the activities using those lines have changed. Agricultural designation, industrial occupation and the movement of environmental burdens are therefore not equivalent geographies.

Reading this condition requires several scales at once: the parcel through which a factory takes root; the wider network of roads, drainage, agriculture and production on which it relies; and the regulatory institutions that address land uses individually. A map of factories alone cannot account for these relationships. Nor can legal recognition of a particular plot by itself demonstrate that the shared landscape functions safely or equitably.

The boundary of ownership is not the boundary of impact.

What appears to be an irregular land-use mosaic is also a durable spatial record of how industrial production has grown through the agricultural landscape.
