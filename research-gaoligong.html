Research / Enquiry 03

A Working Mountain

Ancient Trails, Tea Cultivation & Everyday Life in the Gaoligong Mountains

Heritage / Productive Landscapes / Ecology / Rural Life Dahaoping, Tengchong, Yunnan · 2026 Field research / Historical investigation / Landscape mapping

Opening proposition

A mountain is more than the landscape we see. It is also the history of how people move, work, cultivate and inhabit it.

In the Gaoligong Mountains of western Yunnan, the historic Yongchang Trail has gradually lost its role as a major transport corridor. Yet the surrounding landscape is far from abandoned. Tea cultivation, processing, village life and ecological processes continue to shape the territory.

This research examines the mountain not as a scenic wilderness or a collection of isolated heritage sites, but as a working landscape whose history, economy and ecology remain intertwined. Historical maps are read alongside walking routes, vernacular spaces, forest-floor observations and encounters with people familiar with the tea landscape.

Research scope

Historical review · Territorial and topographic mapping · Field walks and photographic observation · Informal on-site conversations · Documentation of existing tea-production and village spaces

Research question

How does an ancient trading route continue to shape a mountain landscape long after its original transport function has declined?

01 / Historic routes

After the Caravans

A historic corridor transformed by modern transportation

The Yongchang Trail formed part of the wider network of historic overland routes connecting southwestern China with Southeast and South Asia. Crossing the Gaoligong Mountains, the route linked settlements, trading posts and mountain passes through which people, commodities and cultural practices circulated. Its history survives in dispersed physical traces, including former caravan stations, beacon towers, charcoal kilns, stone paths and landmarks associated with the movement of traders and pack animals. [G1]

The opening of the Baoshan–Tengchong road in 1952 marked a major change in regional mobility. According to the historical account assembled in the thesis, motorised transportation increasingly displaced the older caravan network. Sections of the mountain route declined in use, while new roads altered connections between villages and surrounding settlements.

In Dahaoping, this transition can be understood as more than the deterioration of a historic path. The former relationship between movement, settlement and exchange gradually changed. The surviving fragments document both the historical significance of the corridor and the transformation of the territorial system around it.

The following two maps should be read at different scales: the regional overland network establishes the wider geography of movement; the Dahaoping survey identifies surviving local stations, monuments and landscape landmarks.

02 / Agricultural history

From Opium to Tea

Agricultural change as social history

Tea cultivation gives Dahaoping a second historical narrative, one that extends beyond the decline of the caravan route. A local historical account cited in the thesis traces the Feng family's promotion of tea cultivation to approximately 1916. Associated with the movement known as Xing Cha Fei Yan (兴茶废烟, promoting tea cultivation in place of opium), the initiative sought to replace opium production with an alternative agricultural livelihood. [G2]

This history connects a change in cultivation to broader questions of local economy and social reform. Tea was not simply another crop introduced into the mountains; its expansion was associated with a reorganisation of productive activity and the economic identity of the area.

The Fenglaoye Ancient Tea Garden preserves an important part of this narrative. The thesis describes approximately 3,000 mu of old, extensively managed tea woodland, together with facilities associated with tea processing and local tea culture. Its significance cannot be reduced to the age of its trees or its visual character: cultivation also embodies accumulated knowledge of harvesting, processing, climate and terrain.

Research method / Local conversations

Present-day site conversations and annotated mapping helped locate productive spaces, routes and material traces. They complement historical documents but should not be treated as independent proof of the events recorded around 1916.

03 / Productive ecology

A Forest That Is Worked

Tea cultivation within a living ecological landscape

The tea woodland at Fenglaoye differs from the highly regular geometry commonly associated with intensive plantations. Tea trees grow among forest vegetation and across irregular mountain slopes. The site investigation describes a low-input cultivation practice in which the trees are maintained without routine chemical fertilisation, pesticide application or systematic vegetation clearance.

Many tea trees recorded in the study reach approximately two to three metres in height. Their growth patterns make harvesting physically demanding, involving reaching, bending branches and selectively collecting leaves. These observations complicate a simple distinction between natural forest and cultivated land. The garden is neither untouched wilderness nor a landscape entirely reconstructed for agricultural production; its character reflects a continuing interaction between ecological processes and human labour.

The terrain is experienced differently according to the activity taking place. A hillside that appears picturesque to a visitor may be demanding working ground for someone gathering tea leaves. An opening between trees may serve harvesting access rather than represent vacant land. Vegetation mapping alone cannot describe the productive geography of the woodland; seasonal labour, access and maintenance also matter.

Field method / Walking the site

Following the route on foot makes changes in slope, access, vegetation and ground conditions apparent. Walking documents the mountain as an everyday terrain rather than a distant view.

04 / Production and material culture

Where the Leaves Become Tea

Production spaces and material knowledge

Tea production continues beyond harvesting. The site documentation records an existing timber tea-processing workshop containing bamboo withering racks and a traditional masonry stove used in the initial preparation of tea leaves. These ordinary facilities reveal how production is organised around the handling and transformation of harvested material.

The nearby tea house accommodates practices associated with Leixiang Cha (雷响茶), a local tradition of roasting and preparing tea. Together, these places connect agricultural work with local knowledge, hospitality and forms of social exchange.

Timber columns, woven bamboo enclosures, earth walls and rough stone surfaces appear in the existing facilities and settlement fabric. Rather than constituting a single architectural style, these materials reflect practical relationships with available resources, climate and everyday use. A workshop can be significant both for its material character and for the processes it preserves: tools, work surfaces and spatial arrangements document a way of making.

05 / Terrain and climate

The Ecological Ground

Topography, rainfall and the conditions of occupation

Dahaoping lies on the western slopes of the Gaoligong Mountains, at an elevation of approximately 2,000 metres. The mountain range forms part of a globally significant biodiversity region and contains protected ecological areas. The surveyed landscape combines forest habitats, steep topography, seasonal water movement and long-established cultivation. [G3]

GIS-based analysis undertaken for the thesis identifies substantial slope variation, commonly between approximately 15 and 30 degrees across the study area. The northern part of the surveyed tea-garden site is comparatively gentle, while the southern portion contains steeper and more extensive tea woodland. The thesis characterises the regional climate as humid and strongly influenced by the monsoon, with annual precipitation exceeding 1,500 millimetres and much of the rainfall concentrated between May and October.

Persistent moisture, seasonal runoff and temperature variation shape both vegetation and everyday work. Tree roots, forest soils, understory vegetation and drainage patterns form interconnected systems that are not visible in a single plan or photograph. At the same time, established tea cultivation demonstrates a history of human use. Ecology and livelihood are overlapping conditions rather than opposing categories.

The thesis documents these conditions at landscape scale; it does not constitute a comprehensive biodiversity assessment or an independent determination of protected-area boundaries.

06 / Everyday territories

Beyond the Tea Garden

Village life, labour and dispersed places

Dahaoping is not simply a settlement beside a historic trail. Its development has been shaped by transportation, agricultural production and changing relationships with the wider region. The thesis describes a multiethnic village in which tea cultivation and processing remain important elements of economic and social life. [G4]

Cultivation, harvesting, preparing and consuming tea connect the agricultural landscape with domestic routines and places of gathering. The productive geography extends beyond the plantation, encompassing workspaces, processing facilities, routes between sites and areas of everyday exchange.

Historical traces are distributed throughout this wider territory, including the old tea factory, Huangzhuyuanpu (黄竹园铺) and the Fengkou monument (风口大碑). The survey also records ordinary logistical and commemorative places, including loading areas, roads and burial grounds. Such spaces may appear incidental in a visitor-oriented account of the mountain, but they belong to the village's everyday geography.

The village is an inhabited territory in which productive, domestic, commemorative and logistical activities coexist. A fuller account of how they interact would require resident interviews and observation across the agricultural calendar; the thesis provides a spatial investigation rather than a complete ethnographic study.

Research method / Everyday knowledge

The tea house is more than a picturesque structure. It provides a setting for shared practices, informal exchange and conversations about the surrounding landscape. Field photographs document such encounters without replacing a systematic interview study.

07 / Heritage and contemporary use

Heritage Is Not the Same as Tourism

Preservation, visitation and production do not mean the same thing

As interest in cultural routes and rural tourism grows, historic mountain landscapes are increasingly encountered through walking, sightseeing and heritage interpretation. These activities may draw attention to routes that have become marginal within modern transportation networks. They can also change how a productive landscape is understood.

A tea garden viewed primarily as scenery may conceal the work required to sustain it. A historic path promoted as a visitor itinerary may have different significance for residents using nearby routes for everyday movement. A former processing facility may be valued for its atmosphere while its productive history becomes secondary.

In Dahaoping, the legacies of caravan trade, tea production and forest ecology occupy the same territory. The historic route does not have to recover its former commercial role to remain meaningful, but heritage recognition alone does not establish social or economic continuity.

How the history of tea production is represented, whose knowledge is preserved, who benefits from increased visitation and how ecological responsibilities are shared remain questions for further research with residents, agricultural workers, local institutions and conservation authorities. [G5]

08 / Findings and limitations

Reading the Mountain as a Whole

Movement, production and ecology as intersecting histories

The investigation brings together three histories often considered separately: the Yongchang Trail and its changing role after modern transportation; the establishment and continuation of tea cultivation and its material infrastructure; and the mountain's steep terrain, seasonal climate and forest systems within which settlement and production have developed.

These histories do not occupy separate layers. They intersect in paths, workshops, trees, village spaces and the routines through which the territory is used and maintained. The available documentation establishes some of their spatial relationships, while leaving important questions open. Household economic data, systematic interviews, seasonal labour records and comprehensive ecological surveys would be needed to understand how those relationships operate and change over time.

The Gaoligong Mountains are not an empty natural setting surrounding fragments of history. They are a working territory in which the afterlife of trade, the continuity of tea cultivation, everyday village practices and ecological processes remain inseparable.

The research records a living territory, while recognising the limits of a short field investigation. Further seasonal observation, locally grounded oral histories and ecological data could deepen understanding of the relationships documented here.
